1.Why does the Eldora Record exist? On Sept. 30, 2026, the Mayor and Board of Trustees told residents that if the purchase doesn't close, the Town would owe about $2.5 million in transaction costs, which "would be paid through our CSAFE investments" (Town update). Other funds' money comes with limits, whether it moves as a loan or a transfer. The 2025 audit reports all of the Community Center Fund's and the Downtown Development Authority's balances as restricted. The utilities' cash is planned for about $7.9 million in fire-flow improvements in 2030–2031, and their $3,070,436 in state loans is repaid from their revenue and a dedicated share of the sales tax. Repaying $1.6 million to the Water Fund by 2030, before its fire-flow project, would take about $406,000 a year, well above the General Fund's average yearly gain of $126,000 to $199,000. The General Fund has never borrowed from another fund in the audits searched. Whether a loan or transfer is allowed, and whether a multi-year loan needs voter approval under TABOR, is a question for the Town attorney or auditor. Bankruptcy and dissolving aren't ways out. Colorado law allows municipal bankruptcy only for irrigation and drainage districts, and a town can dissolve only after providing for its debts. By the record's reading, this is a budget problem the Board must close over later budgets, by cutting or delaying spending, negotiating with the firms, selling Town property (park land needs voter approval), or asking voters for a tax.

8. 4.Could the Town go bankrupt or dissolve? How would a deficit be paid off? How would the budget cover it?

The Town's own forecast, presented Nov. 9, 2026, charges its Eldora estimate to the General Fund and has the fund Nov. 9, 2026, forecast has the General Fund ending 2026 at −$1,948,512 and 2027 at −$2,132,122, for in a fund that spends about $3.2 million a year. With the record's itemized total in place of the Town's estimate, total, 2026 would end at about −$2,068,634.

Neither bankruptcy nor dissolving would remove the costs. A town can file for municipal bankruptcy only if its state authorizes it, and Colorado's statute names only irrigation and drainage districts. A town can dissolve only after providing for all its debts, levying taxes if needed. By the record's reading, this is a General Fund budget problem for the Board to address through its budgets, not a threat to the Town's existence. State law doesn't allow a budget that spends more than the money available, so the Board would have to close the gap over later budgets. Its options include cutting or delaying spending (which could mean fewer staff or reduced services), negotiating payment terms with the firms, selling Town property (land used for parks needs voter approval), or asking voters to approve a new tax or multi-year borrowing. The TABOR emergency reserve can't be used for a revenue shortfall. At the General Fund's past rate of gains, rebuilding the Town's forecast deficit of about $1.9 million would take roughly 10 to 15 years. That is a measure of scale, not a forecast: the Town also forecasts the fund spending more than it takes in during 2026 and 2027, before Eldora costs. A Nov. 16 staff memo proposes transfers, not loans, of up to $800,000 from the Community Center Fund and up to $2.3 million from the Water and Sewer Funds, described as "user fees," recorded in an amended 2026 budget on Dec. 1. Voters approved the Community Center's sales tax for the Community Center, most recently in 2022, and the Town's books list that fund as fully restricted; voters dedicated the utilities' sales tax to their state loans. By the record's check, the memo's 75% share from Sewer exceeds Sewer's cash, and the plan closes only if nearly all of the money in the three funds can be used. Whether the transfers are lawful is a question for the Town Attorney. The 2027 budget hearing is Dec. 1, and adoption is set for Dec. 8.

9. 5.How does this meet the 2027 budget, and who is managing it? What does Resolution 2026-31 require?

The Board began discussing the 2027 budget at its Nov. 9, 2026, meeting. Staff forecast the General Fund deficit above and offered three ways to cover it, all using other Town funds' money: folding the Community Center Fund into the General Fund (up to $0.9 million), a transfer from the Water and Sewer Funds “with no expectation of repayment” (up to $2.0 million), or dissolving the Downtown Development Authority (about $1.9 million). Staff said it would still need to assess how much of each is restricted. The Board agreed to have staff research the options. The full draft budget is scheduled for Nov. 17, a public hearing Dec. 1, and adoption Dec. 8. Draft Resolution 2026-31 would have the Town Manager present all of a 2026 appropriation for the costs, a 2027 budget line, and a plan for the General Fund if the acquisition doesn't close. The Town Manager resigned in August 2026. The previous Town Manager returned as interim on Sept. 25, under a contract through Feb. 28, 2027, and says the budget is her priority, to "identify potential opportunities for expenditure reductions and alternative revenue sources." Recruiting a permanent Town Administrator is expected to begin after the 2027 budget is developed. The Town Treasurer retired in 2026, and since July 2026 the Town's monthly treasurer reports have been issued by Plante Moran as Town Treasurer; the Town Manager's Nov. 17 report lists filling the Town Treasurer position among her priorities. An Oct. 20 staff memo says the Eldora work "is primarily being performed by the Mayor" and that "the Town is not paying outside consultants to perform this work." adopted it Nov. 17, 2026, and it took effect that day. It's the Town Attorney's revision of a resident's draft. It:

  • stops billable work by the firms, except toward a term sheet, with written notice due by Nov. 20;
  • allows up to $80,000 toward a term sheet, only after the Board appropriates it, and requires a vote to continue if total costs pass $3 million;
  • requires Board approval of any new engagement;
  • requires monthly cost reports starting Dec. 1 and a firm-by-firm accounting at the Dec. 15 meeting;
  • requires budget items for the costs and a General Fund plan if the deal fails; and
  • sets deadlines the Board can extend only by a vote before each passes: a term sheet by Feb. 14, 2027, a signed agreement and financing by April 14, and a closing by June 30. If one passes without an extension, the Town's pursuit ends.

6.Where does the deal stand, and who would run the resort?

No purchase agreement is in force. The April 30, 2026, closing deadline passed, and the Town said Sept. 30 that the agreement "has expired." No bonds have been sold, and the Board's authority to sell them ran out July 19, 2026. The Town says talks with POWDR "remain active." Its new plan is a sale-leaseback, in which a bond trustee would hold title and lease the resort back to the Town's enterprise; on Oct. 22 the interim Town Manager wrote that the lease "has not been drafted." Any new agreement, lease, or bond ordinance would need Board approval at a public meeting.

The Town dropped its planned operating partner, 303 Ski, by April 2026 and said it would rely on Eldora's existing management, then "professional on-mountain management" after a two-year transition agreement with POWDR.

11.What would bondholders control if the purchase closes? The bonds would be repaid only from the resort's income. The January 2026 draft indenture says they "shall not constitute or become an indebtedness, a debt, or a liability or a charge against the general credit or taxing power of the Town." The draft bond documents also set terms on how the Town and the resort would operate while the bonds are outstanding. The May 2026 draft, the newest the Town has released, includes these terms, some still marked as tentative: An independent consultant would approve the resort's annual budgets. The Town couldn't end POWDR's transition agreement early without bondholder consent. The January draft of that agreement would let the Town end it on 60 days' notice. The Town would keep its corporate existence and not seek bankruptcy or dissolution. Revenue left after operations, debt payments, and reserves would go first to paying off the senior bonds early. Money in the Surplus Fund after that "may be used for any lawful purpose by the Town." Part of the price would be paid in subordinate bonds "delivered to or at the direction of the Seller," with the investor letter drafted for POWDR. They pay no current interest, grow in value until paid, and are paid only after the senior bonds, beginning in November 2036. Under the sale-leaseback the Town described in October 2026, the bond trustee would hold title to the land and buildings and lease the resort back to the Town's enterprise. If the bonds defaulted, the trustee could exercise its remedies "without first completing a traditional foreclosure process," and "those remedies could affect who operates or leases the resort." Title would return to the enterprise once the bonds are paid. The lease and the final bond terms aren't public, and they may differ from the drafts. 13.Where does the deal stand, and who would run the resort? No purchase agreement is in force. The closing deadline of April 30, 2026, passed without a closing, and the Town said Sept. 30 that the agreement "has expired."

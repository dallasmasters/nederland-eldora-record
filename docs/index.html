Why I made this page

I built this page while researching the Town's public records on its website and CivicWeb portal. Like many residents, my views on the Eldora acquisition have changed over time, but from the start I worried about the cost of pursuing it and the risk to the Town if the deal fell through, since those costs would be repaid only if the sale closed. This page tracks those costs from the Town's own records and gathers information scattered across thousands of documents, to help us figure out the best path forward.

My opinions about what will happen and what the Town should do are on a separate page, so that they stay apart from the record. Because the sale is still more likely to fail than close, I recommend that the Board halt spending on the acquisition, keep firm deadlines for ending the effort, and focus on the Town's finances and the 2027 budget. I've drafted a resolution that would do that. On my rough review of the numbers, any future attempt should start from a cash price of about $70 million or less.

— Dallas Masters, Nederland resident · Questions and corrections: corrections@eldorarecord.org

The acquisition in brief

POWDR put Eldora Mountain Resort, the ski area just west of Nederland, up for sale in 2024. The Town plans to buy it through a new Town-owned business, the Mountain Recreation Enterprise, and pay with revenue bonds repaid only from the resort's income, not taxes. In the May 2026 draft bond documents, part of the price would have been paid in bonds to POWDR, repaid only after the senior bonds. A January 2026 draft agreement would also have the Town pay POWDR at least $1 million a year for two years of transition services after closing. Its stated reasons are local control and long-term revenue (Ord. 873, FAQ 6.0), including "addressing recurring budget shortfalls through a more stable revenue base" (FAQ 7.0). The plan is being restructured (see below).

  1. November 2024: the Town said it was pursuing the purchase, and on Nov. 19 the Board first discussed it in open session, as a discussion item with no vote (memo, minutes). Closed sessions on purchasing “property for recreational activities” began Sept. 3, 2024 (Board votes).
  2. July 2025: non-binding term sheet signed with POWDR (FAQ 3.0).
  3. January 2026: Board approved a $120 million purchase and up to $225 million in bonds (Ord. 882, Ord. 885).
  4. April 30, 2026: the purchase agreement's closing deadline passed without a closing (APA §7.1(d), FAQ 7.0).
  5. July 19, 2026: the Board's authority to sell the bonds ran out with no bonds sold (Ord. 885 §5(c)).
  6. September 30, 2026: the Town said the agreement had expired but it is still pursuing the purchase with restructured financing (Town update).

Where things stand. No purchase agreement is in force. The Town remains bound by its January 2025 confidentiality agreement with POWDR, and a few terms of the expired agreement continue, including that each side pays its own costs (APA §7.2, §8.2). The Town's September 30 update describes a revised plan: a "sale-leaseback structure tied to the asset," less debt, and about $20 million in philanthropy for capital projects, with the State of Colorado the first potential contributor, "at the beginning stages" (Town update). An overview dated Oct. 16, 2026, in the Oct. 20 Board packet describes the structure. At closing, the Enterprise would transfer legal title to the land and improvements to the bond trustee, which would lease the resort back to the Enterprise to operate. If the bonds defaulted, the trustee could exercise the remedies in the financing documents "without first completing a traditional foreclosure process," and "those remedies could affect who operates or leases the resort." Title would return to the Enterprise once the bonds are paid (sale-leaseback overview). An Oct. 20 staff memo says the Town is pursuing the purchase "at the previously negotiated purchase price of $120 million" and exploring "revenue bonds, governmental grants, foundation funding, and private contributions." It says this work "is primarily being performed by the Mayor" and that "the Town is not paying outside consultants to perform this work" (Oct. 20 memo). Stifel's advisory agreement, extended in July 2026, covers municipal leases as well as bonds (agreement). The Town Manager resigned in August 2026, and the Town's previous Town Manager returned as interim Town Manager on Sept. 25, 2026 (Aug. 18 minutes, p. 4); her contract runs through Feb. 28, 2027, and recruiting a permanent Town Administrator is expected to begin after the 2027 budget is developed (Oct. 20 report, reply to public comment). Not in the records searched: the lease terms, a new bond amount, and any pledges. Responding to a records request on Oct. 22, 2026, the interim Town Manager wrote that the lease “has not been drafted” and provided the newest draft indenture the Town has, dated as of May 1, 2026, before the sale-leaseback plan (Town response). A new agreement, lease, or bond ordinance would each need Board approval at a public meeting.

Where the money comes from

Every Eldora acquisition bill to date was charged to the General Fund and paid from the Town's pooled cash (see CSAFE), recorded in the "Due From" account as money owed back to the Town. Res. 2025-17 states the intent to repay it from bond proceeds, and the August 2025 legal-counsel memo says those legal fees would be "funded from the bond issuance proceeds." The bonds would pay for the purchase, improvements, reserves, early interest, and issuance costs, and repay the Town's early costs; no taxes or General Fund money are pledged (Ord. 885). The financing is being restructured (where things stand). In January 2025 the Board approved sending Great Outdoors Colorado a letter of interest about a possible grant (minutes); no award appears in the records searched.

The Town created a separate fund for the acquisition, but the records searched don't show it being used. Ordinance 873 (Jan. 21, 2025) established the Mountain Recreation Fund as a TABOR enterprise to "be kept separate from the Town's general fund and other funds," and its staff memo listed "Feasibility Studies and Planning" and costs of "potential acquisition efforts" among its uses (Ord. 873, memo). If the General Fund ends in deficit

The General Fund has already relied on other funds' cash: the 2025 audit reports its "negative cash balance of $757,482" as accounts payable, covered by the Town's pooled cash (2025 audit). Colorado law sets the rules a deficit would face. A local government's budget may not "provide for expenditures in excess of available revenues and beginning fund balances," and no one may spend or contract to spend "in excess of the amounts appropriated" (C.R.S. 29-1-103(2), 29-1-110). So paying these costs would need a Board appropriation, and a budget that starts from a deficit would have to close it with more revenue, less spending, or transfers. For scale, $1.3 million is about 40% $1.7 million is about 52% of the General Fund's 2026 operating budget of $3,236,824, and $2.5 million is about 77% $2.9 million is about 89% (budget summary). Under TABOR, the Town needs voter approval in advance for "any new tax, tax rate increase," or mill levy increase, and for any "multiple-fiscal year direct or indirect district debt or other financial obligation" not backed by cash reserves pledged for all future payments. The TABOR emergency reserve, $147,000 of the General Fund's balance, is "for declared emergencies only," and an emergency "excludes economic conditions, revenue shortfalls" (Colo. Const. art. X, §20(2)(c), (4), (5)). At the General Fund's 2017–2023 average gain of $126,000 to $199,000 a year, rebuilding about $1.9 $2.3 million would take roughly 10 to 15 11 to 18 years (this page's calculation). That is a measure of scale, not a forecast: the General Fund's balance fell by $265,953 in 2024 and $192,241 in 2025, and the adopted 2026 budget projects a further decline of $446,348 (year by year).

The State Auditor's fiscal-health benchmark for counties and municipalities is an unrestricted General Fund balance of at least two months of expenditures, a ratio of 0.167, and it says a negative unrestricted balance "is not sustainable, and a government should address the situation as soon as possible" (State Auditor, 2013, p. 13). Nederland's ratio was about 0.26 at the end of 2025; if the sale fails in 2026, it would be about −0.64 −0.75 (this page's calculation). In the audits searched from 2008 through 2024, the General Fund appears as a lender to other funds, not a borrower; in 2008 it "temporarily covered the negative cash balances" of two other funds (2008 audit), and 2025 is the first year an audit reports it with negative cash. The Town's formal loans between funds have gone to the Downtown Development Authority, not the General Fund (see below). The records searched don't show how the budget law's limit applies when a fund begins the year with a negative balance, which read literally could require closing the gap within one budget year; that is a question for the Town attorney or auditor.

The costs would still be owed; FAQ 7.0 says some contracts “will need to be paid regardless of closing.” In practice, the pooled cash would pay the bills first, as it already covers the General Fund's negative cash, and the General Fund would owe that money back. Under the budget law above, the Board would then have to close the gap in later budgets: by cutting or delaying spending, which could mean fewer staff or reduced services; by negotiating payment terms with the firms; by selling Town property, such as land; or by asking voters to approve a new tax or multi-year borrowing. Bankruptcy and dissolution. Neither would remove these costs. A local government can file for federal municipal bankruptcy (Chapter 9) only if its state specifically authorizes it (U.S. Courts). The Colorado statute that does so names only an “irrigation or drainage district” (C.R.S. 37-32-102), and no statute searched authorizes a town. A town can end its incorporation only if 25% of its registered voters petition and two-thirds vote to do so, and only after it “has made proper provisions for the payment of all of its indebtedness” and its contracts, levying taxes if needed (C.R.S. 31-3-101, 31-3-104). A town that goes five years without holding elections or maintaining a government can be declared abandoned, but its debts “shall not be abrogated,” and the county takes over to pay them (C.R.S. 31-3-201, 31-3-202). Excess cash flow "does not become meaningful until the subordinate bonds are paid off," which the Town aimed to do "within ten years." "After that, free cash flow will fall between $2M - $5M+." Annexing the resort "could add $1M - $2M in annual revenue to the Town's general fund." The FAQ said it "cannot disclose exact figures" (FAQ 3.0).
  • August 2025. FAQ 4.0: after the subordinate bonds are retired and reserves are built, the Board could direct "excess revenues" to streets, sidewalks, affordable housing, and childcare (FAQ 4.0).
  • April 2026. FAQ 6.0 no longer gives the annexation estimate. It says much of the ski area is on Forest Service land that "is not anticipated" to be annexed, and that annexation "must be evaluated separately" (FAQ 6.0).
  • September 2026. The Town update gives no revenue or profit figures. It says the first "approximately ten years" are "more financially constrained," and that its assumptions have become more conservative (Town update).

No financial model, feasibility study, or bond-offering document is in the records searched. Under the draft Indenture of Trust (January 20, 2026, packet), revenue left after operations, debt payments, and reserves goes first to paying off the senior bonds early, all of it until revenue covers debt payments two to one for three straight years, then half. What remains goes to a "Surplus Fund" that stays available to pay the bonds; that draft has no provision releasing money to the Town (Indenture §5.07, §5.11). The May 2026 draft keeps the early-payoff rule, returns it to 100% after any draw on the reserves, and says money in the Surplus Fund "may be used for any lawful purpose by the Town" (May draft §5.07, §5.11, §5.12; Town response). The final terms may differ.

The May 2026 draft also shows part of the price being paid in bonds. Subordinate "capital appreciation" bonds were to be "delivered to or at the direction of the Seller in consideration for a portion of the purchase price," with the investor letter drafted for the "initial purchase by Powdr Corp." These bonds pay no current interest; their value grows, compounding every six months. They can't be transferred before Oct. 1, 2036, have no reserve, and are paid only after the senior bonds and required deposits, beginning in November 2036 (May draft §2.05, §2.06, §3.02, §5.07, Exhibit I). In the January draft, the subordinate bonds were to be sold for cash. The Town materials searched mention subordinate bonds but not that the seller would hold them. Other May draft terms, some still marked as tentative: the senior bonds would be sold only to institutional investors, without a rating, at no more than 9%; bond proceeds would pay interest until about April 2029, with added deposits of $1.2 million each March from 2027 to 2029; principal payments would begin about November 2035; and the senior bonds would mature by 2039 and 2056. An independent consultant would approve the resort's annual budgets; the Town couldn't end POWDR's transition agreement early without bondholder consent, though the January draft of that agreement would let the Town end it on 60 days' notice (draft agreement §5.03); and while the bonds are outstanding, the Town would keep its corporate existence and not seek bankruptcy or dissolution (§6.10, §6.11, §6.16, §6.17). All bond amounts and rates are blank (May draft, Town response).

Records
Open records requests go to the Town Clerk. The first hour of staff time is free; more is charged at a rate capped by state law (C.R.S. 24-72-205). A named document is faster and cheaper than a keyword search; see the records list. Executive-session discussions and confidential documents may be withheld. Minutes are the official record; verbatim transcripts are made only on request, for a fee (2026 fees).
Referendum
Registered voters can petition to send most ordinances to a public vote. The petition needs signatures from 5% of registered voters within 30 days after the ordinance is published, and the ordinance is on hold until the Board repeals it or voters decide (C.R.S. 31-11-105). Residents used this in 2024 on Ordinance 859, the TEENS child care approval (Town memo). This applies to ordinances approving a purchase or bonds, even though the bonds themselves need no election.
Initiative
Registered voters can propose an ordinance by a petition signed by at least 5% of registered voters. The Board must adopt it unchanged within 20 days or send it to the voters (C.R.S. 31-11-104).
Elections
The next regular municipal election is in April 2028 (Town memo). A vacant trustee seat was filled by appointment in September 2026 (Res. 2026-25). Colorado's constitution also allows recall of elected officials by petition (Colo. Const. art. XXI).

Nederland is a statutory town, and it applied these state petition rules in 2024. The Board has discussed converting to home rule, which would let a charter set different rules (Sept. 15 minutes). About these figures

  • Why the account is treated as Eldora acquisition spending. It first appears on the Oct. 7, 2025, warrant list, right after the Board hired Eldora due-diligence consultants, whose costs were to be "reimbursed from revenue bond proceeds" (Res. 2025-19, 2025-22). Other reimbursable work, such as FEMA, CDOT, and DDA projects, has its own accounts, and no charge in the account was found to belong to another program. The Town's spreadsheet of the account's 2025–2026 entries, released in response to a public records request, is titled "Eldora - reimbursable expenses," and the entries moved into the account are labeled "Eldora Reclass" (ledger).
  • The total covers only bills booked to the "Due From" account, which opened in August 2025. It includes $56,743 in invoices the Town moved into the account from the Legal and Professional Services budget lines (see Who was paid), among them Widner Juran invoices from January to July 2025. Any other Eldora acquisition work charged to ordinary budget lines and not moved isn't identified in the records searched and is not included.
  • The Town moved the earlier invoices in one journal entry dated Sept. 30, 2025, eight weeks after the Board declared its intent to reimburse acquisition costs from bond proceeds (Res. 2025-17, Aug. 5, 2025). The entry includes a Dickinson Wright invoice first paid on the Oct. 7, 2025, warrant list (p.3), so it was made after Sept. 30 and dated to September (ledger).
  • Most invoices dated in 2025 but paid in 2026 appear in the Town's 2025 ledger, including two entries recorded as accruals on Dec. 31, 2025. Three do not: a Plante Moran invoice dated Dec. 4, 2025 ($69,000), and two Partner Assessment Corporation invoices dated Sept. 30 and Dec. 31, 2025 ($43,000). The ledger lists them only in 2026; they were paid on the July 21 and Oct. 6, 2026, warrant lists, the longest gaps between invoice and payment in the account (ledger).
  • Totals are by warrant-list date, when the Board approved payment. Invoice dates are often earlier; some invoices dated in 2025 were paid in 2026, so the totals show when bills were approved, not when work was done.
  • The running balance printed on each warrant list matches the listed invoices, except for the amounts added between lists (shown under Who was paid) and one Widner Juran invoice ($16,985) that reached the balance one list before it was listed. Corrected Oct. 11, 2026
  • Amounts are rounded to the nearest dollar, so a total can differ by a dollar from the sum of its rounded parts. The data files in the project repository keep the exact amounts.

How this research was done

This page was researched and built by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, working in Claude Code at the direction of Dallas Masters. The research began in October 2026. Claude collected, read, and analyzed the documents. The researcher chose the questions and decided what went on the page.

  • Collecting the records. All 16,352 documents listed in the Town's CivicWeb Document Center were cataloged. 6,606 of them were selected and downloaded:
    • Board of Trustees agendas, packets, agenda attachments, and minutes from January 2023 on, including executive-session agendas and the February 2026 town hall on the acquisition.
    • Agendas, packets, and minutes of the Town's other boards and commissions from January 2024 on: the Downtown Development Authority, Planning Commission, Sustainability Advisory Board, Parks, Recreation, and Open Space Advisory Board, and Board of Zoning Adjustment (1,356 documents).
    • All years of resolutions, ordinances, finance documents, and staff reports.
  • Reading the text. Text was taken directly from each PDF where possible. The 9,242 pages that were scanned images were read with optical character recognition (OCR, the Tesseract program). All 64,574 pages went into a searchable database, so every finding can be traced to a document and page.
  • Tracing the payments. Every warrant list (the bills the Board approves at each meeting) was parsed for payments to the General Fund account 10-00-1150, "Due From." The account's running balance printed on each list was reconciled against the listed invoices. That check found three amounts posted between lists. The Town's ledger for the account, from a public records response, identifies the entries that make up each one; it was checked entry by entry against the warrant lists, including the earlier lists where moved invoices were first paid.
  • Checking each payee. Every payee was searched for across the whole archive, including contracts, engagement letters, staff memos, and minutes. Their other Town billings were compared to see whether "Due From" charges were kept separate. Each payee was then rated by whether a Board document states its role in the Eldora acquisition.
  • Reading the contracts. The draft Asset Purchase Agreement, the Transition Services Agreement, the bond ordinance and the delegation resolutions were read for cost-sharing, refund, termination, and spending limits.
  • The Town's website. The Town's Eldora FAQs and announcements are posted on its website; only FAQ Round 2 also appears in CivicWeb, in the Jan. 7, 2025, Board packet. Current posts (FAQ 6.0, FAQ 7.0, the September 30, 2026, update, and the auditor request for proposals) came from nederlandco.gov. Earlier FAQs were on the Town's previous website, which no longer serves them, so they came from the Internet Archive. FAQ 5.0 could not be found. The 2022, 2023, and 2025 audits are also posted only on the Town website. They are scanned PDFs and were read with OCR. All were added to the same searchable database as the Board records.
  • Records from public records requests. Records the Town released in response to an open records (CORA) request are used like the Town's other records. The Town's ledger for account 10-00-1150, "Due From," came from its Sept. 25, 2026, response to a resident's request, which the requester shared with this project. The ledger matches every warrant-list payment on this page and the account's running balance to the cent. The ledger and the Town's response are posted with this page; the requester's name and contact details are not. These records were added to the same searchable database as the Board records.
  • Meeting videos. The Town posts recordings of Board meetings on its YouTube channel. To link each decision to the moment it comes up in the recording, the recordings of the meetings with Eldora decisions were transcribed by machine (whisper.cpp), and YouTube's automatic captions were searched for other meetings. The transcripts were used only to find those times, and each link was checked by watching the recording; they are not a source for anything on this page, because they are unofficial and contain errors. The minutes are the official record.
  • Outside sources. State statutes and the Colorado Constitution are cited for CSAFE, for the budget and TABOR rules a General Fund deficit would face, and under Get involved; they come from the official 2024 Colorado Revised Statutes. The State Auditor's 2013 fiscal-health analysis for counties and municipalities is cited for its General Fund reserve benchmark. The section on property taxes uses Boulder County records: the Assessor's account data (Oct. 17, 2026, download), its 2025 mill levy file and certifications of value to each district, and the Treasurer's account pages for the three largest accounts, printed Oct. 27, 2026. Accounts were matched to the sellers by owner name and mailing address. Everything else on this page comes from the Town's own records, website, and Facebook page. FAQ 7.0 refers to a BusinessDen article; the article itself is not used as a source.
  • Reading the minutes. The signed "Minutes - Adopted" scans carry a built-in text layer that often garbles names and votes. All 123 adopted Board minutes since 2023 were therefore re-read with OCR at high resolution. Names were checked against the page images wherever they were unclear. Four sets of minutes were never posted as separate adopted minutes; for those (Oct. 21, 2025, regular and special, Oct. 30, 2025, and the Sept. 15, 2026, special meeting), the drafts in the next meeting's packet were used.
  • Finding the motions. 500 motions were extracted with mover, seconder, outcome, roll call, absences and late arrivals. Votes on the acquisition were chosen by reading each candidate item's staff memo and attachments, because a search for "Eldora" alone also returns items that mention it only in passing. Every executive session whose motion cites the purchase of property for recreational activities was included.
  • Checking for gaps. Agendas were checked for executive sessions on the purchase that have no motion in any minutes. Those are listed separately, along with one whose agenda is marked canceled. No minutes were found for four special meetings (Dec. 23, 2024, and May 30, July 21, and July 29, 2025).
  • Limits. When this page says something isn't in the records, it means the records searched; it may be documented elsewhere. OCR can misread scanned text, and an AI can misread or misjudge a document. Every figure and claim on this page links to its source document, so check those before relying on them. Executive sessions are confidential and the public record has gaps, so this page cannot show everything the Board knew or decided.

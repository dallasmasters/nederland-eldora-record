Nederland's Eldora acquisition: summary, costs, and where the money is coming from

As of the Oct. 20, 2026, warrant list, the Town had paid $1,195,949 in Eldora acquisition costs, mostly to lawyers and consultants, all charged to its General Fund as money it expects to recover from revenue bonds if the purchase closes. The purchase agreement expired in April 2026, and no bonds have been sold; the Town says it is pursuing a restructured deal. If the sale doesn't close, the Town estimates it would owe about $2.5 million in all, paid from its CSAFE investments; it hasn't itemized that estimate, and no budget or dollar limit covers the costs. By this page's calculation, if the sale fails in 2026, the General Fund would end the year about $1.9 million below zero. This page compares those amounts with the Town's finances, lists what the records searched don't show, and records every Board vote on the acquisition, each unanimous among the trustees present.

This is an independent compilation, not affiliated with, endorsed by, or produced by the Town of Nederland, Eldora Mountain Resort, or POWDR. It is built from the Town's own public records: Board of Trustees meeting records from Jan. 3, 2023, through Oct. 20, 2026, on the Town's CivicWeb portal; resolutions, ordinances, finance documents, and staff reports from all years; posts on the Town website; and Town records released in response to a public records request. Statements that something isn't in the records refer only to the records searched (see How this research was done). Last updated Oct. 17, 2026; the page is updated about once a month, and sooner when there is significant news. Corrections are welcome: corrections@eldorarecord.org.

Why I made this page

I built this page as a byproduct of researching the Town of Nederland's public records, available on the Town website and its CivicWeb portal of official Town documents. Like many residents, my opinions on the Eldora acquisition have fluctuated and evolved over time. But from day one I have been worried about the cost of the acquisition process itself and the financial risk to the Town if the deal didn't go through, since the due-diligence costs would be repaid from bond proceeds only if the sale closed. So I have tried to do objective research, based only on the Town's records, to explain how much the acquisition process has and will cost and how much financial risk it poses to the Town. I still don't pretend to know the best path forward, but I hope this page's careful accounting, and its summary of information scattered across thousands of documents, can help us figure it out. out the best path forward.

My opinions about what will happen and what the Town should do are on a separate page, so that they stay apart from the record.

— Dallas Masters, Nederland resident · Questions and corrections: corrections@eldorarecord.org

The acquisition in brief

POWDR put Eldora Mountain Resort, the ski area just west of Nederland, up for sale in 2024. The Town plans to buy it through a new Town-owned business, the Mountain Recreation Enterprise, and pay with revenue bonds repaid only from the resort's income, not taxes. Its stated reasons are local control and long-term revenue (Ord. 873, FAQ 6.0), including "addressing recurring budget shortfalls through a more stable revenue base" (FAQ 7.0). The plan is being restructured (see below).

  1. July 2025: non-binding term sheet signed with POWDR (FAQ 3.0).
  2. January 2026: Board approved a $120 million purchase and up to $225 million in bonds (Ord. 882, Ord. 885).
  3. April 30, 2026: the purchase agreement's closing deadline passed without a closing (APA §7.1(d), FAQ 7.0).
  4. July 19, 2026: the Board's authority to sell the bonds ran out with no bonds sold (Ord. 885 §5(c)).
  5. September 30, 2026: the Town said the agreement had expired but it is still pursuing the purchase with restructured financing (Town update).

Where things stand. No purchase agreement is in force. The Town remains bound by its January 2025 confidentiality agreement with POWDR, and a few terms of the expired agreement continue, including that each side pays its own costs (APA §7.2, §8.2). The Town's September 30 update describes a revised plan: a "sale-leaseback structure tied to the asset," less debt, and about $20 million in philanthropy for capital projects, with the State of Colorado the first potential contributor, "at the beginning stages" (Town update). An overview dated Oct. 16, 2026, in the Oct. 20 Board packet describes the structure. At closing, the Enterprise would transfer legal title to the land and improvements to the bond trustee, which would lease the resort back to the Enterprise to operate. If the bonds defaulted, the trustee could exercise the remedies in the financing documents "without first completing a traditional foreclosure process," and "those remedies could affect who operates or leases the resort." Title would return to the Enterprise once the bonds are paid (sale-leaseback overview). An Oct. 20 staff memo says the Town is pursuing the purchase "at the previously negotiated purchase price of $120 million" and exploring "revenue bonds, governmental grants, foundation funding, and private contributions." It says this work "is primarily being performed by the Mayor" and that "the Town is not paying outside consultants to perform this work" (Oct. 20 memo). Stifel's advisory agreement, extended in July 2026, covers municipal leases as well as bonds (agreement). The Town Manager resigned in August 2026, and the Town's previous Town Manager returned as interim Town Manager on Sept. 25, 2026 (Aug. 18 minutes, p. 4); her contract runs through Feb. 28, 2027, and recruiting a permanent Town Administrator is expected to begin after the 2027 budget is developed (Oct. 20 report, reply to public comment). Not in the records searched: the lease terms, a new bond amount, and any pledges. A new agreement, lease, or bond ordinance would each need Board approval at a public meeting.

Where the money comes from

Every Eldora acquisition bill to date was charged to the General Fund and paid from the Town's pooled cash (see CSAFE), recorded in the "Due From" account as money owed back to the Town. Res. 2025-17 states the intent to repay it from bond proceeds, and the August 2025 legal-counsel memo says those legal fees would be "funded from the bond issuance proceeds." The bonds would pay for the purchase, improvements, reserves, early interest, and issuance costs, and repay the Town's early costs; no taxes or General Fund money are pledged (Ord. 885). The financing is being restructured (where things stand). In January 2025 the Board approved sending Great Outdoors Colorado a letter of interest about a possible grant (minutes); no award appears in the records searched.

Can the Town absorb it?

In the accounts, the costs are carried by the General Fund, the Town's general operating fund. This section compares the costs with the Town's households, funds, and budgets.

Could the Town pay the rest?

If the Town's $2.5 million estimate includes what it has already paid, about $1.3 million more would be owed if the sale fails; if it doesn't, the full $2.5 million would be owed on top of what has been paid. The records searched answer only part of whether the Town could pay it. The cash exists: the Town's pooled cash and investments totaled $7,154,592 at the end of 2025, including $2,821,014 in CSAFE (2025 audit). But most of it belongs to funds restricted to the utilities, the Downtown Development Authority, the community center, and streets and parks. The General Fund is the only fund that could pay these costs, and only with a Board appropriation, since neither the 2025 nor the 2026 budget includes them (the Town's funds, budgets). By this page's calculation, the General Fund's own share of the pool was about $698,751 at the end of 2025, and it has since paid $825,108 in Eldora acquisition costs, more than that share; the records searched don't show its current position. This page's reading is that paying $1.3 million more would draw on pooled cash belonging to other funds, and that writing off the costs already paid as well would leave the General Fund's balance about $1.5 million below zero (year by year zero, or about $1.9 million below zero with the projected 2026 deficit (tables below). The records searched don't show the current balances, whether other funds' money may legally carry these costs (a question for the Town attorney or auditor), what the $1.3 million consists of or when it would be due, or which reserves FAQ 7.0 called "sufficient." Staff plan to review Eldora-related expenditures and the FY2026 and FY2027 budgets at the Nov. 5, 2026, budget work session (Oct. 20 memo).

Why the fund balance can look healthier than the cash. When the Town pays an Eldora acquisition bill from the General Fund, the cash leaves either way, but the books can record the payment in one of two ways. Recorded as spending, it reduces the General Fund's balance right away. Recorded as a receivable, it counts as money the Town expects to get back, so the balance on paper doesn't change, much like a personal loan that hasn't been repaid yet. The Town's account name, "Due From," and its ledger title, "Eldora - reimbursable expenses," suggest the $1,195,949 paid so far is carried as a receivable, to be repaid from bond proceeds if the purchase closes; the Town hasn't said so directly. The records are consistent with that reading. The 2025 audit shows the General Fund's "other receivables" rising from $6,431 to $520,055 during 2025, without itemizing them, while the account reached $370,841 on the warrant lists ($504,396 in the Town's ledger, which includes 2025 invoices paid in 2026; ledger). If the General Fund ends in deficit

The General Fund has already relied on other funds' cash: the 2025 audit reports its "negative cash balance of $757,482" as accounts payable, covered by the Town's pooled cash (2025 audit). Colorado law sets the rules a deficit would face. A local government's budget may not "provide for expenditures in excess of available revenues and beginning fund balances," and no one may spend or contract to spend "in excess of the amounts appropriated" (C.R.S. 29-1-103(2), 29-1-110). So paying these costs would need a Board appropriation, and a budget that starts from a deficit would have to close it with more revenue, less spending, or transfers. Under TABOR, the Town needs voter approval in advance for "any new tax, tax rate increase," or mill levy increase, and for any "multiple-fiscal year direct or indirect district debt or other financial obligation" not backed by cash reserves pledged for all future payments. The TABOR emergency reserve, $147,000 of the General Fund's balance, is "for declared emergencies only," and an emergency "excludes economic conditions, revenue shortfalls" (Colo. Const. art. X, §20(2)(c), (4), (5)). At the General Fund's 2017–2023 average gain of $126,000 to $199,000 a year, rebuilding about $1.9 million would take roughly 10 to 15 years (this page's calculation). That is a measure of scale, not a forecast: the General Fund's balance fell by $265,953 in 2024 and $192,241 in 2025, and the adopted 2026 budget projects a further decline of $446,348 (year by year).

The State Auditor's fiscal-health benchmark for counties and municipalities is an unrestricted General Fund balance of at least two months of expenditures, a ratio of 0.167, and it says a negative unrestricted balance "is not sustainable, and a government should address the situation as soon as possible" (State Auditor, 2013, p. 13). Nederland's ratio was about 0.26 at the end of 2025; if the sale fails in 2026, it would be about −0.64 (this page's calculation). In the audits searched from 2008 through 2024, the General Fund appears as a lender to other funds, not a borrower; in 2008 it "temporarily covered the negative cash balances" of two other funds (2008 audit), and 2025 is the first year an audit reports it with negative cash. The Town's formal loans between funds went to the Downtown Development Authority, authorized by Board resolution with repayment from the authority's tax increment revenue; one cites a 2012 election in which voters authorized borrowing up to $913,589, "repayable only from tax increment revenues" (Res. 2015-06). The records searched don't show whether, or on what terms, each fund could lend to the General Fund, whether a loan between Town funds repaid over several years would need voter approval, or how the budget law's limit applies when a fund begins the year with a negative balance, which read literally could require closing the gap within one budget year; those are questions for the Town attorney or auditor.

Per household

Nederland has about 685 households (Sept. 15, 2026, packet) and 1,471 residents (2020 Census, parks plan). The $1,195,949 paid so far is about $1,746 per household. The Town's $2.5 million estimate is about $3,650 per household ($1,700 per resident), or about $5,396 if the estimate doesn't include what's already paid. No one would be billed these amounts; they only show scale. Sales tax is about 40% of the General Fund's budgeted 2026 revenue, not counting grants (budget summary).

The CSAFE investments and the Town's funds

CSAFE (the Colorado Surplus Asset Fund Trust) is a state-regulated investment pool that works like a money-market fund (C.R.S. 24-75-701). It is not set aside for any purpose. In March 2023 the Board approved investing "surplus funds... not immediately required to be disbursed" there (Res. 2023-16, on the consent agenda): $1.5 million then and $1 million more in January 2024. The Treasurer can withdraw "as funds are needed" without a new vote. The balance was $2,700,831 at the end of 2024 and $2,821,014 at the end of 2025 (2025 audit); the monthly treasurer reports don't show its current balance.

The Town pools its cash, so the CSAFE money belongs to every Town fund, not to one. Most of it comes from funds restricted by law or contract.

The Mayor calls for comment on each action and discussion item, and there is a time for comment on topics not on the agenda. Comments are limited to 3 minutes; speakers state their name and whether they live in Nederland.

Records
Open records requests go to the Town Clerk. The first hour of staff time is free; more is charged at a rate capped by state law (C.R.S. 24-72-205). A named document is faster and cheaper than a keyword search; see the records list. Executive-session discussions and confidential documents may be withheld. Minutes are the official record; verbatim transcripts are made only on request, for a fee (2026 fees).
Referendum
Registered voters can petition to send most ordinances to a public vote. The petition needs signatures from 5% of registered voters within 30 days after the ordinance is published, and the ordinance is on hold until the Board repeals it or voters decide (C.R.S. 31-11-105). Residents used this in 2024 on Ordinance 859, the TEENS child care approval (Town memo). This applies to ordinances approving a purchase or bonds, even though the bonds themselves need no election.
Initiative
Registered voters can propose an ordinance by a petition signed by at least 5% of registered voters. The Board must adopt it unchanged within 20 days or send it to the voters (C.R.S. 31-11-104).
Elections
The next regular municipal election is in April 2028 (Town memo). A vacant trustee seat was filled by appointment in September 2026 (Res. 2026-25). Colorado's constitution also allows recall of elected officials by petition (Colo. Const. art. XXI).

Nederland is a statutory town, and it applied these state petition rules in 2024. The Board has discussed converting to home rule, which would let a charter set different rules (Sept. 15 minutes). About these figures

  • Why the account is treated as Eldora acquisition spending. It first appears on the Oct. 7, 2025, warrant list, right after the Board hired Eldora due-diligence consultants, whose costs were to be "reimbursed from revenue bond proceeds" (Res. 2025-19, 2025-22). Other reimbursable work, such as FEMA, CDOT, and DDA projects, has its own accounts, and no charge in the account was found to belong to another program. The Town's spreadsheet of the account's 2025–2026 entries, released in response to a public records request, is titled "Eldora - reimbursable expenses," and the entries moved into the account are labeled "Eldora Reclass" (ledger).
  • The total covers only bills booked to the "Due From" account, which opened in August 2025. It includes $56,743 in invoices the Town moved into the account from the Legal and Professional Services budget lines (see Who was paid), among them Widner Juran invoices from January to July 2025. Any other Eldora acquisition work charged to ordinary budget lines and not moved isn't identified in the records searched and is not included.
  • Totals are by warrant-list date, when the Board approved payment. Invoice dates are often earlier; some invoices dated in 2025 were paid in 2026, so the totals show when bills were approved, not when work was done.
  • The running balance printed on each warrant list matches the listed invoices, except for the amounts added between lists (shown under Who was paid) and one Widner Juran invoice ($16,985) that reached the balance one list before it was listed. Corrected Oct. 11, 2026
  • Amounts are rounded to the nearest dollar, so a total can differ by a dollar from the sum of its rounded parts. The data files in the project repository keep the exact amounts.
  • Source: Board of Trustees warrant lists (Council Approval Reports) on the Town's CivicWeb portal, through the October 20, 2026, meeting, and the Town's ledger for the account through Sept. 25, 2026, released in response to a public records request (ledger, Town response) and, for 2026, posted in the Oct. 20, 2026, Board packet (packet copy).

How this research was done

This page was researched and built by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, working in Claude Code at the direction of Dallas Masters. The research began in October 2026. Claude collected, read, and analyzed the documents. The researcher chose the questions and decided what went on the page.

  • Collecting the records. All 16,297 documents listed in the Town's CivicWeb Document Center were cataloged. 5,211 of them were downloaded:
    • Board of Trustees agendas, packets, agenda attachments, and minutes from January 2023 on, including executive-session agendas and the February 2026 town hall on the acquisition.
    • All years of resolutions, ordinances, finance documents, and staff reports.
    Other boards and commissions were left out at the requester's direction.
  • Reading the text. Text was taken directly from each PDF where possible. The 7,979 pages that were scanned images were read with optical character recognition (OCR, the Tesseract program). All 51,586 pages went into a searchable database, so every finding can be traced to a document and page.
  • Tracing the payments. Every warrant list (the bills the Board approves at each meeting) was parsed for payments to the General Fund account 10-00-1150, "Due From." The account's running balance printed on each list was reconciled against the listed invoices. That check found three amounts posted between lists. The Town's ledger for the account, from a public records response, identifies the entries that make up each one; it was checked entry by entry against the warrant lists, including the earlier lists where moved invoices were first paid.
  • Checking each payee. Every payee was searched for across the whole archive, including contracts, engagement letters, staff memos, and minutes. Their other Town billings were compared to see whether "Due From" charges were kept separate. Each payee was then rated by whether a Board document states its role in the Eldora acquisition.
  • Reading the contracts. The draft Asset Purchase Agreement, the Transition Services Agreement, the bond ordinance and the delegation resolutions were read for cost-sharing, refund, termination, and spending limits.
  • The Town's website. The Town's Eldora FAQs and announcements are posted on its website; only FAQ Round 2 also appears in CivicWeb, in the Jan. 7, 2025, Board packet. Current posts (FAQ 6.0, FAQ 7.0, the September 30, 2026, update, and the auditor request for proposals) came from nederlandco.gov. Earlier FAQs were on the Town's previous website, which no longer serves them, so they came from the Internet Archive. FAQ 5.0 could not be found. The 2022, 2023, and 2025 audits are also posted only on the Town website. They are scanned PDFs and were read with OCR. All were added to the same searchable database as the Board records.
  • Records from public records requests. Records the Town released in response to an open records (CORA) request are used like the Town's other records. The Town's ledger for account 10-00-1150, "Due From," came from its Sept. 25, 2026, response to a resident's request, which the requester shared with this project. The ledger matches every warrant-list payment on this page and the account's running balance to the cent. The ledger and the Town's response are posted with this page; the requester's name and contact details are not. These records were added to the same searchable database as the Board records.
  • Meeting videos. The Town posts recordings of Board meetings on its YouTube channel. To link each decision to the moment it comes up in the recording, the recordings of the meetings with Eldora decisions were transcribed by machine (whisper.cpp), and YouTube's automatic captions were searched for other meetings. The transcripts were used only to find those times, and each link was checked by watching the recording; they are not a source for anything on this page, because they are unofficial and contain errors. The minutes are the official record.
  • Outside sources. State statutes are cited for CSAFE and under Get involved. and the Colorado Constitution are cited for CSAFE, for the budget and TABOR rules a General Fund deficit would face, and under Get involved; they come from the official 2024 Colorado Revised Statutes. The State Auditor's 2013 fiscal-health analysis for counties and municipalities is cited for its General Fund reserve benchmark. Everything else on this page comes from the Town's own records and website. FAQ 7.0 refers to a BusinessDen article; the article itself is not used as a source.
  • Reading the minutes. The signed "Minutes - Adopted" scans carry a built-in text layer that often garbles names and votes. All 123 adopted Board minutes since 2023 were therefore re-read with OCR at high resolution. Names were checked against the page images wherever they were unclear. Four sets of minutes were never posted as separate adopted minutes; for those (Oct. 21, 2025, regular and special, Oct. 30, 2025, and the Sept. 15, 2026, special meeting), the drafts in the next meeting's packet were used.
  • Finding the motions. 500 motions were extracted with mover, seconder, outcome, roll call, absences and late arrivals. Votes on the acquisition were chosen by reading each candidate item's staff memo and attachments, because a search for "Eldora" alone also returns items that mention it only in passing. Every executive session whose motion cites the purchase of property for recreational activities was included.
  • Checking for gaps. Agendas were checked for executive sessions on the purchase that have no motion in any minutes. Those are listed separately, along with one whose agenda is marked canceled. No minutes were found for four special meetings (Dec. 23, 2024, and May 30, July 21, and July 29, 2025).
  • Limits. When this page says something isn't in the records, it means the records searched; it may be documented elsewhere. OCR can misread scanned text, and an AI can misread or misjudge a document. Every figure and claim on this page links to its source document, so check those before relying on them. Executive sessions are confidential and the public record has gaps, so this page cannot show everything the Board knew or decided.

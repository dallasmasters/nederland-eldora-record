My opinions on the Eldora purchase

Dallas Masters, Nederland resident · Oct. 17, 2026; revised Oct. 29, 2026

Everything else on this site is drawn from Town records and official sources and stands on its own. This page is my opinion. The facts it relies on are linked to the Eldora Record, where you can check them and reach your own conclusions. With the sale still about three times as likely to fail as to close, I recommend that the Board halt spending on the acquisition now, keep firm deadlines for ending the effort, and focus on the Town's finances and the 2027 budget. I've drafted a resolution that would do that, and I suggest the Board adopt it at its Nov. 9, 2026, meeting. On the rough review below, the resort's reported earnings can support a cash price of about $70 million or less, and the proposed sale-leaseback is highly unlikely to make the deal work at $120 million on its own. For $120 million to work, Eldora would need revenue of roughly $56 million to $70 million a year, well above the roughly $50 million reported (revenue needed); any future deal should start from what the resort's verified earnings support and include an independent review.

What the Town would owe if the deal fails

As of mid-October, the Town had paid about $1.2 million, and firms had confirmed it owes about $1.7 million more, for about $2.9 million in all (who was paid and who is owed). That's about $4,200 per household, and about 89% of the General Fund's yearly operating budget. By the Eldora Record's calculation, the General Fund would end 2026 about $2.3 million below zero.

And the number keeps growing:

The first figure counts only what had been paid; the other two are totals. But they all point the same way, and some costs continue as long as the effort does.

  • My biggest concern is what the Town would owe if the deal doesn't close, and that the amount keeps growing. If the deal fails, it is increasingly looking like the Town will end 2026 with the General Fund at about −$2.3 million, a very large deficit for a town whose General Fund spends about $3.2 million a year, against an already bleak financial forecast (the Town's own 2026 budget deficit). This will likely result in borrowing from other funds and some combination of cutting non-essential services and staff, selling Town assets, and having little to no discretionary funding for many years. That figure uses the itemized total above. In my view, it is a better guide than the Town's unitemized $2.5 million estimate, and it may still be low, since several firms that have billed the Town sent no confirmation. Much of that cost hasn't appeared on the warrant lists or in the Town's ledger. The warrant lists the Board approved showed about $1.2 million. The firms' own confirmations show the Town owes nearly $1.7 million more, most of it to the Town's real estate counsel, whose agreement bills only “upon completion or termination of the transaction” (engagement agreement). And as long as the effort continues, so do some of the costs: the Town Attorney billed Eldora work for September, and the data room is billed every month (who was paid and who is owed). Some bills won't arrive until the deal ends.
  • The Town Board did not have bad intentions, but it has put the Town at significant and unprecedented financial risk. I also think the Town's budgeting and accounting for the acquisition costs raise serious questions: the costs were charged to the General Fund rather than the enterprise fund created for the acquisition, left out of the 2025 and 2026 budgets, and carried as money to be repaid from bonds the Town is no longer authorized to sell. That is why the draft resolution asks for an independent accounting review.
  • The idea of owning Eldora is attractive, but it might still be a daunting undertaking and risky even if the deal finalizes. Under the May 2026 draft of the bond documents, an independent consultant would approve the resort's budgets, the Town couldn't end POWDR's transition agreement early, and money would reach the Town only after the resort earned twice its senior bond payments for three straight years, far more than the 1.25 times the review below finds it can support.
  • We got here by what looks like groupthink about the attractiveness of the idea and promises of long-term financial gain, without much questioning of the financial costs should the deal not go through. This is evidenced by lots of discussion about the costs being covered by the eventual bond proceeds but very little about what happens if the deal falls through, and about the increasing probability that it won't happen. In the meetings I reviewed, the only time a trustee asked what the Town would owe if the deal didn't close was on Jan. 7, 2025 (video). After that, in the minutes and recordings of the open meetings I reviewed, no trustee asked about the running cost totals, which appeared on the warrant lists they approved with the consent agenda (the running total), or about the financial risk if the sale failed. The questions that did come up were about which costs could be reimbursed, and the Town's first estimate of what it would owe came in its Sept. 30, 2026, update.
  • The deal is more likely to fail than close. My current rough estimate is about a 25% probability that it closes, and well under that if Eldora's revenue is near the published figures. This is due to:
    • a price gap: on the rough review below, the resort's reported earnings support a cash price of about $70 million or less. The Town hasn't named a source of outside money for the difference; the roughly $20 million in philanthropy it described, with the State of Colorado as the first prospect, is for capital projects (Sept. 30 update), and the state cut programs this year to close a shortfall of roughly $1.5 billion (KUNC). According to the Colorado Sun, which cited a source with that buyer, POWDR rejected another buyer's bid below $100 million in 2024
    • financing getting harder because of rising interest rates, with prospective bondholders already turning down the first deal's terms (where things stand)
    • staffing issues: the town manager who shepherded the deal left, an interim town manager has taken over, the treasurer position is open, and the current work is "primarily being performed by the Mayor" without paid consultants (Oct. 20, 2026, memo)
    • nothing keeping POWDR from selling to another buyer (the purchase agreement has expired)
    • many instruments needing to be redone: a new purchase agreement, a new or amended bond ordinance, and new bond documents, for a sale-leaseback that on its own is unlikely to close the price gap; as of Oct. 22, the Town said the lease "has not been drafted" (Town response)

    The estimate multiplies my rough odds for each step the deal still needs, each assuming the steps before it succeed, in three cases for what Eldora's revenue turns out to be (revenue needed), then weights the cases. All three assume POWDR is paid $120 million, the price it accepted in January and the Town is still pursuing.

    The weights are my judgment. The Town's plan to borrow $180 million (Res. 2025-17) and SE Group's early comment that there “appears to be sufficient NOI to serve the planned debt” point to earnings above the published estimate. The bonds not selling, buyers asking for poor snow years to be modeled more aggressively, and the Town's “more conservative” assumptions since then point the other way. POWDR isn't certain to sign again: the first agreement expired, the structure is changing, and it is free to sell to someone else. In a thin case, a bad snow year could still put the bonds near the default line. Plante Moran's report on Eldora's earnings would show which case applies.

  • A rough review of the numbers that I made with help from Claude (below) suggests that in a normal year Eldora's earnings could about cover bonds equal to the $120 million price, but not the larger borrowing the Town planned, and not a run of bad seasons. On that review, the deal would be viable with total borrowing of about $90 million to $95 million, which points to a cash price of about $70 million or less.
  • With the deal still about three times as likely to fail as to close, the Town Board should halt spending on it now, with firm limits (draft resolution):
    • no new acquisition costs, and no new engagements, without a Board vote
    • a final accounting from each firm of what the Town owes, separating what is owed now from what would be owed only at a closing
    • a firm deadline: a signed purchase agreement and committed financing by Jan. 31, 2027, and closing by April 30, 2027, one year after the original closing deadline, with the effort ending if either is missed
    • an independent review of the final terms before any vote, testing whether the resort can carry the debt at current interest rates and through bad snow years without help from the Town
    • regular public accounting of the costs and of progress on the deal
    The final accounting is doable: on Oct. 19, the Town asked the firms what they were owed, and it had their answers within four days. Halting spending doesn't reduce what the Town already owes, but it keeps the total from growing and lets the Board and staff put their attention on the budget. In any case, the Board should plan the 2027 budget for the deal failing. The costs paid so far are carried as money the Town expects to recover from bond proceeds, but the purchase agreement has expired and the authority to sell the bonds lapsed on July 19, 2026, so that repayment depends on agreements and a bond ordinance that don't yet exist. I think the 2027 budget should show these costs as General Fund spending, to be recovered as revenue if the bonds are ever sold.

Can Eldora's earnings pay for the bonds? A rough review

In short: under reasonable assumptions, including bad snow years, Eldora's reported earnings would about cover bonds equal to the $120 million price in a normal year, with nothing to spare. The reserves in the Town's draft bond documents, about $17 million at that price, could carry a few bad winters, but a single year with visits down about 10% would already be a default under those documents, and the $180 million the Town expected to borrow wouldn't be covered at all. The modeled deal becomes viable with total borrowing of about $90 million to $95 million. Because the bonds also pay for reserves, interest, and other costs, that points to a cash price of about $70 million or less, and closer to $50 million if about three years of interest is paid from the bonds, as the Town's draft bond documents provide. For the $120 million plan to work, Eldora's revenue would need to be roughly $56 million to $70 million a year, rather than about $50 million (What revenue would the $120 million plan need?).

I suggested this analysis; Claude, Anthropic's AI assistant, helped prepare it. Karen Blakemore, a reader, supplied an independent ChatGPT analysis that reached similar results. It's a rough review, not a forecast: the Town's model and SE Group's study would be more precise, but neither is public.

How long would reserves last? With visits down 25%, then 15%, then 5%, here's what would be left at $120 million, starting from the $10 million reserve the Town planned (FAQ 4.0) and from the reserves in the May 2026 draft: a year of senior bond payments (about $10.6 million) plus an operating reserve of $6.2 million, about $16.8 million in all.

The larger reserves in the draft would last, but they wouldn't prevent a default. The draft makes any year in which earnings fall below the senior bond payments a default, even if the reserves pay the bondholders (§6.20). At $120 million, a normal year is already at about that line, and a year with visits down 10% falls below it. A default would let bondholders accelerate the bonds, seek a receiver, and direct changes in how the resort is run, with money still in the reserves. Drawing on the reserves would also send all spare cash back to early repayment, and they would have to be refilled before capital projects (§5.07, §5.12). In roughly the first three years, interest paid from the bonds themselves would cover the payments, so early bad years would strain operations more than the bond payments.

At what price would the modeled deal work?

The price paid to POWDR and the amount borrowed are different. The Town expected to borrow $180 million for a $120 million price (Res. 2025-17); the rest would pay for the reserve, interest during the first years, issuance costs, starting deposits, and capital projects. So the review first asks how much the resort could borrow, then subtracts those other costs.

Viable here means earnings at least 1.25 times the bond payments in a normal year, the minimum in the Town's January and May draft bond documents (draft indenture §6.20, May 2026 draft). That allows bond payments of about $8.4 million a year ($10.5 million ÷ 1.25), which at 8.5% repays about $95 million over 40 years, or about $90 million over the 30 years the drafts set for the main bonds. The May draft pays only interest until about 2035 and repays principal from then to 2056, which puts it near the low end.

The result depends most on the earnings estimate, which comes from a trade report, not Eldora's books. For a $120 million price to work from borrowing alone, Eldora would need to earn about $18 million to $21 million a year before interest and depreciation, rather than $12.5 million, or roughly 45% to 65% more. If earnings were 20% higher or lower than the estimate, the cash price would be about $90 million or about $45 million.

By cash price I mean what POWDR could be paid at closing from the bonds the resort can support. The Town is still pursuing the purchase at $120 million (Oct. 20 memo), which leaves a gap of about $50 million to $70 million. It would have to come from outside money (the Town has named about $20 million in philanthropy for capital projects, Sept. 30 update); from POWDR taking part of the price in seller bonds, as in the May 2026 draft, where POWDR would take subordinate bonds it couldn't sell before 2036, whose value grows until they're paid from what the resort has left after the senior bonds and required deposits; or from higher earnings, since each $1 million a year of actual earnings above the reported figures supports about $9 million more borrowing.

The draft would also make any calendar year with earnings below the bond payments a default, even if the reserve covers the payments (§6.20). At about $90 million, one year with visits down 10% to 20% could do that. Both drafts predate the sale-leaseback plan, and as of Oct. 22 the Town said the lease "has not been drafted" (Town response). Either price is below the bid POWDR rejected in 2024, according to the Colorado Sun. Outside money lowers the borrowing needed dollar for dollar, so the borrowing figure is the steadier target.

Could better bond terms, such as those a sale-leaseback might bring, close the gap on their own? A $120 million price plus about $25 million to $40 million in other costs needs roughly $145 million to $160 million of borrowing.

Even the last row falls just short, and it would take rates about 2½ points lower, the longest term the Board allowed, and a thinner safety margin all at once. Bond buyers have pushed the other way, asking the Town “to more aggressively model poor snow years” (Sept. 30 update). The trustee holding title would help bondholders after a default, but it wouldn't make a bad winter less likely. What would change the odds is a much lower price or much more outside money, not the financing structure. So in my view, a sale-leaseback that closes the gap on its own is highly unlikely.

What revenue would the $120 million plan need?

Eldora's actual revenue isn't public, and it may be higher than the trade-report estimate this review starts from. So here is the question turned around: how much revenue would Eldora need in a normal year for a $120 million price to work from borrowing alone?

Bond termsEarnings neededRevenue needed if most costs are fixedRevenue needed at a flat 25% margin
8.5%, 30 years (the drafts)$18.9M–$20.6M$60M–$70M$76M–$82M
8.5%, 40 years$18.0M–$19.7M$59M–$68M$72M–$79M
7.5%, 40 years (illustrative; no sale-leaseback terms have been published)$16.4M–$17.9M$56M–$64M$66M–$72M

Each row assumes borrowing of $145 million to $160 million (the price plus other costs, above), $2 million a year of upkeep, and earnings of at least 1.25 times the bond payments. “Most costs fixed” uses the same rule as the bad-year cases, run in reverse: each added dollar of revenue adds 40 to 64 cents of earnings. A flat 25% margin is the upper bound, if costs grew as fast as revenue. Compared with the roughly $50 million estimate, that is about 12% to 40% more revenue, or 32% to 64% more at a flat margin. Earnings just equal to the bond payments, the default line in the drafts, would take about $52 million to $61 million. The roughly $20 million in planned philanthropy is for capital projects (Sept. 30 update), so it doesn't reduce this borrowing.

That makes the question concrete: does Eldora's revenue reach about $56 million to $70 million in a normal year, and hold up well enough in a bad one to stay above the default line? Plante Moran's quality-of-earnings report, prepared for the Town from Eldora's books, would show it, but it isn't public. If the answer is yes, the deal looks much better than this review suggests; if revenue is near $50 million, no bond terms modeled here make $120 million work.

How this compares with SE Group's study

SE Group, the Town's due-diligence consultant, had Eldora's actual books, but its projections “typically stop at the EBITDA line level” (proposal); its report isn't public. This review picks up where that stops: whether the earnings can carry the debt. Before its review, SE Group wrote that “there appears to be sufficient NOI to serve the planned debt, but a strategic plan will additionally be needed” for maintenance and improvements. That matches this review at $120 million.

How the review works
  • Two cases for bad years. Milder: 30% of revenue doesn't change with visits, and 40% of operating costs fall with visits, so each 10% drop in visits cuts earnings about $2 million. Harsher, based on Karen Blakemore's ChatGPT analysis: revenue falls 80% as fast as visits and costs fall little, about $3.2 million per 10% drop.
  • Ikon. Sources told The Colorado Sun that 60% to 75% of Eldora's lift-ticket revenue comes from Alterra's Ikon payments (Colorado Sun); the purchase agreement describes that money as payable “for scans” (APA §2.2, §8.13). The review assumes the Ikon agreement keeps its current terms. The current agreement is among POWDR, Snowbird Resort, and Alterra, so the Town would need its own agreement for Eldora (APA definitions, §3.2(f)).
  • Bad years aren't far-fetched. Colorado Ski Country USA's preliminary figures put statewide skier visits at 10.5 million in 2025–26, down 24% from 13.9 million, in a low-snow winter. SAM also noted that advance-purchase season passes kept revenue at many resorts from falling as much as visits (SAM). Eldora's own numbers aren't public.
  • Bonds. The Town now models rates “closer to 8.5 percent.” Real bonds wouldn't have equal payments; the January plan included bonds with later payments, which ease early years but cost more later. The bonds would be repaid only from the resort; if the reserve ran out, remedies would be the bondholders' (where things stand).
  • The price. The price is the borrowing less the other costs paid from the bonds. The draft bond documents would also fund a CapEx fund, a capital maintenance fund, and an operating reserve at closing, with the deposits left blank (draft indenture §3.02, §5.05); the May 2026 draft sets an operating reserve of $11.7 million at closing and a CapEx requirement of $47.5 million. It also measures coverage after administrative costs, including POWDR's transition fees and amounts owed to Alterra, which this review doesn't subtract, so the review may overstate what the resort can borrow. Upkeep is the Town's $2 million a year, which doesn't cover new lifts or lodges. The price is an estimate of what the reported earnings could support, not a Town or POWDR figure, and says nothing about what POWDR would accept.

The records that would settle these questions, including SE Group's report, the bond payment schedule, and the amounts set aside at closing, are on the Eldora Record's list of records anyone can request. This is also why I think the Board should require an independent review that tests the final terms at current interest rates and through bad snow years before any vote.

My opinions on the Eldora purchase

Dallas Masters, Nederland resident · Oct. 17, 2026; revised Nov. 15, 18, 2026

After Nov. 17: what comes next

The Board did the right thing on Nov. 17. Resolution 2026-31 puts limits on Eldora spending for the first time: no more billable work except toward a term sheet, no money for that work until the Board appropriates it, a vote if costs pass $3 million, monthly public reports, and deadlines that end the effort unless the Board votes to extend them.

The resolution limits what happens next. It doesn’t pay for what has already happened. The Town still owes roughly $2.9 million, paid and owed, all charged to the General Fund, and the 2026 budget has to be amended before Dec. 31 to account for it. That decision, on Dec. 1, matters more than anything the Board did on Nov. 17.

The Town’s official Eldora updates are still brief and short on detail, and none shows significant progress. The Nov. 17 update answers one resident’s question about the bonds and lists two documents of ideas for donations and partners. It says nothing about the status of talks with POWDR, a term sheet, the financing, or the lease, which as of Oct. 22 hadn’t been drafted. To me, that supports what I’ve expected: the risk is growing that the deal will fail and that the costs will never be covered by a bond sale.

On that decision, my views haven’t changed:

  • I don’t think the Town can use most of the Community Center money. Voters approved that tax for the Community Center as recently as 2022, and the Town’s own books list the fund as fully restricted (what voters approved).
  • Water and Sewer money should move only as far as the Town Attorney confirms in writing it lawfully can, and only down to sensible reserves, not to zero. As proposed, the plan takes more from Sewer than Sewer has (the record’s check).
  • The 2027 budget needs real cuts. The Sheriff’s contract, about $930,000 a year, is the only single item big enough to matter, and reducing or ending it is a very good idea that should be on the table (below). Selling Town land can help rebuild reserves.

Above all, the fund transfers should be a last resort, and the Board needs to get serious about finding ways to offset them. Taking residents’ water, sewer, and community center money to cover a ski-area deal will likely lead to calls for resignations, and possibly recalls. Before it gets there, the Board should be looking at cutting almost all discretionary spending. Yet so far it has seen only fund-level totals. It hasn’t been presented the individual departmental budgets to review line by line. Time is running out quickly: the 2026 amendment is Dec. 1 and the 2027 budget Dec. 8. The Board needs to ask for those budgets now, get serious, and start thinking outside the box.

Finally, the Board should fill its open seat immediately, even at its next meeting. These decisions deserve a full Board.

Residents can weigh in at the Dec. 1 budget hearing and the Dec. 8 adoption.

Why it’s hard to stop

The way the costs were recorded created a trap. As long as the Town keeps kept pursuing the deal, the $1.3 million it has paid can had paid could be counted as money it will would get back. The moment it stops, stopped, that money and the $1.6 million still owed would become a loss, with no cash of its own to cover it. That makes continuing look cheaper than stopping, even when the odds say otherwise. Researchers call this escalation of commitment, or the sunk-cost trap.1,2 The way out isn’t a bigger bet. It’s a budget that plans for the deal failing. The Town’s Nov. 9 forecast now counts the costs as General Fund spending, which I think is the right first step: it takes away the accounting reason to keep going, and leaves the real question, how the budget covers them. One part of the trap remains: the real estate counsel bills its roughly $1.2 million only when the deal is completed or ends, so keeping the effort alive postpones the largest bill.

Research on why organizations keep investing in failing projects recommends limits set in advance, a fresh decision at each step, holding decision makers accountable for how they decide rather than only for the outcome, and reviewers who weren’t part of the original decision.3,4 My amendments do each of these, and they make the costs visible every month.

  • I think cutting or ending the Sheriff’s contract is a very good idea that should be on the table as a long-term way to save money, especially if the county keeps basing deputies in Nederland. Law enforcement is the General Fund’s largest line, $1,249,399 in the 2026 budget, and the Sheriff’s contract is $931,629 of it (2026 contract). The Town dissolved its police department in 2023 after it couldn’t keep officers, and it has contracted with the Sheriff since May 2023 (May 2025 memo). In 2024 the contract cost $932,199. For 2025, rather than pay $1,054,037 to keep the same coverage, the Town cut coverage to about 50%, so that deputies spend half their uncommitted patrol time in town, for $885,798; the 2026 contract, at that level, is $931,629, for three deputies, a sergeant, half a detective, and a fifth of an animal control officer (Oct. 2024 memo, Nov. 2024 budget presentation, pp. 26–27, 2025 budget, p. 36). The Town has already shown it can cut this contract. Boulder County contracts with only three towns: Lyons, Nederland, and Superior (Sheriff’s Office). Ward and Jamestown, much smaller mountain towns, have no contract; the Sheriff covers them as part of its regular patrol, and county dispatch answers their 911 calls (county dispatch). Nederland isn’t Ward: it has about 690 households, a commercial center on two state highways, heavy visitor traffic, a municipal court, and Town laws someone has to enforce. But the Sheriff would still answer emergencies and serious crimes here without a contract. What the contract buys is deputies assigned to town, faster response, routine patrol, follow-up on local cases, traffic and Town Code enforcement, and animal control. And because Nederland is the natural base for the Sheriff’s western mountain patrol, with a substation already in town, the county would likely keep deputies here even without a contract, though they would answer unincorporated calls first and wouldn’t enforce Town laws. The Board should ask what the contract adds beyond that baseline, and what it’s worth now. I’d ask the Sheriff what Nederland would get with no contract and to price each step above it for 2027, including dropping the half-time detective and one deputy, which would save about $258,000 a year, roughly the General Fund’s yearly operating gap, and I’d have staff confirm the notice the 2023 agreement requires to change or end it. Ending the contract would save more, up to about $930,000 a year less lost fines and the cost of code and animal enforcement, and over ten years that is more than any other single cut the Town could make. Residents should have a say, since it changes public safety and part of the 2022 sales-tax increase was approved for law enforcement. Restarting a Town police department, which the Board considered in 2025 at an estimated $1 million or more to start and $1 million to $2 million a year to run, isn’t affordable now. The Board’s own strategic plan already calls for deciding “the Town’s law enforcement service and funding model” (strategic plan); the 2027 budget is the time to do it.

Can Eldora's earnings pay for the bonds? A rough review

In short: under reasonable assumptions, including bad snow years, Eldora's reported earnings would about cover bonds equal to the $120 million price in a normal year, with nothing to spare. The reserves in the Town's draft bond documents, about $17 million at that price, could carry a few bad winters, but a single year with visits down about 10% would already be a default under those documents, and the $180 million the Town expected to borrow wouldn't be covered at all. The modeled deal becomes viable with total borrowing of about $90 million to $95 million. Because the bonds also pay for reserves, interest, and other costs, that points to a cash price of about $70 million or less, and closer to $50 million if about three years of interest is paid from the bonds, as the Town's draft bond documents provide. For the $120 million plan to work, Eldora's revenue would need to be roughly $57 million to $64 million a year, rather than about $50 million (What revenue would the $120 million plan need?).

The draft makes any year in which earnings fall below the senior bond payments a default, even if the reserves pay the bondholders (§6.20). At $120 million, a normal year is already at about that line, and a year with visits down 10% falls below it. A default would let bondholders accelerate the bonds, seek a receiver, and direct changes in how the resort is run, with money still in the reserves. Drawing on the reserves would also send all spare cash back to early repayment, and they would have to be refilled before capital projects (§5.07, §5.12). In roughly the first three years, interest paid from the bonds themselves would cover the payments, so early bad years would strain operations more than the bond payments.

At what price would the modeled deal work?

The price paid to POWDR and the amount borrowed are different. The Town expected to borrow $180 million for a $120 million price (Res. 2025-17); the rest would pay for the reserve, interest during the first years, issuance costs, starting deposits, and capital projects. So the review first asks how much the resort could borrow, then subtracts those other costs.

Viable here means earnings at least 1.25 times the bond payments in a normal year, the minimum in the Town's January and May draft bond documents (draft indenture §6.20, May 2026 draft). That allows bond payments of about $8.4 million a year ($10.5 million ÷ 1.25), which at 8.5% repays about $95 million over 40 years, or about $90 million over the 30 years the drafts set for the main bonds. The May draft pays only interest until about 2035 and repays principal from then to 2056, which puts it near the low end.

The result depends most on the earnings estimate, which comes from a trade report, not Eldora's books. For a $120 million price to work from borrowing alone, Eldora would need to earn about $18 million to $21 million a year before interest and depreciation, rather than $12.5 million, or roughly 45% to 65% more. If earnings were 20% higher or lower than the estimate, the cash price would be about $90 million or about $45 million.

By cash price I mean what POWDR could be paid at closing from the bonds the resort can support. The Town is still pursuing the purchase at $120 million (Oct. 20 memo), which leaves a gap of about $50 million to $70 million. It would have to come from outside money (the Town has named about $20 million in philanthropy for capital projects, Sept. 30 update); from POWDR taking part of the price in seller bonds, as in the May 2026 draft, where POWDR would take subordinate bonds it couldn't sell before 2036, whose value grows until they're paid from what the resort has left after the senior bonds and required deposits; or from higher earnings, since each $1 million a year of actual earnings above the reported figures supports about $9 million more borrowing.

The draft would also make any calendar year with earnings below the bond payments a default, even if the reserve covers the payments (§6.20). At about $90 million, one year with visits down 10% to 20% could do that. Both drafts predate the sale-leaseback plan, and as of Oct. 22 the Town said the lease "has not been drafted" (Town response). Either price is below the bid POWDR rejected in 2024, according to the Colorado Sun. Outside money lowers the borrowing needed dollar for dollar, so the borrowing figure is the steadier target.

Could better bond terms, such as those a sale-leaseback might bring, close the gap on their own? A $120 million price plus about $25 million to $40 million in other costs needs roughly $145 million to $160 million of borrowing.

Even the last row falls just short, and it would take rates about 2½ points lower, the longest term the Board allowed, and a thinner safety margin all at once. Bond buyers have pushed the other way, asking the Town “to more aggressively model poor snow years” (Sept. 30 update). The trustee holding title would help bondholders after a default, but it wouldn't make a bad winter less likely. What would change the odds is a much lower price or much more outside money, not the financing structure. So in my view, a sale-leaseback that closes the gap on its own is highly unlikely.

What revenue would the $120 million plan need?

Eldora's actual revenue isn't public, and it may be higher than the trade-report estimate this review starts from. So here is the question turned around: how much revenue would Eldora need in a normal year for a $120 million price to work from borrowing alone?

Each row assumes borrowing of $145 million to $160 million (the price plus other costs, above), $2 million a year of upkeep, and earnings of at least 1.25 times the bond payments. “Most costs fixed” uses the same rule as the bad-year cases, run in reverse: each added dollar of revenue adds 57 to 80 cents of earnings. A flat 25% margin is the upper bound, if costs grew as fast as revenue. Compared with the roughly $50 million estimate, that is about 14% to 28% more revenue, or 44% to 64% more at a flat margin. Earnings just equal to the bond payments, the default line in the drafts, would take about $53 million to $58 million. The roughly $20 million in planned philanthropy is for capital projects (Sept. 30 update), so it doesn't reduce this borrowing. Corrected Nov. 5, 2026: this table first used 40 to 64 cents of earnings per added dollar of revenue, which doesn't match the bad-year rule it describes. With 57 to 80 cents, the revenue needed is lower: about $57 million to $64 million, not $56 million to $70 million. The table and the revenue cases also now use only the 8.5% rate the Town models, dropping an illustrative 7.5% case. The Mountain-Ear commentary of Nov. 5 used the earlier range.

That makes the question concrete: does Eldora's revenue reach about $57 million to $64 million in a normal year, and hold up well enough in a bad one to stay above the default line? Plante Moran's quality-of-earnings report, prepared for the Town from Eldora's books, would show it, but it isn't public: POWDR has taken the position that it is confidential in its entirety, and the Town has asked POWDR to reconsider (Town response). If the answer is yes, the deal looks much better than this review suggests; if revenue is near $50 million, no bond terms modeled here make $120 million work.

How this compares with SE Group's study

SE Group, the Town's due-diligence consultant, had Eldora's actual books, but its projections “typically stop at the EBITDA line level” (proposal); its report isn't public. This review picks up where that stops: whether the earnings can carry the debt. Before its review, SE Group wrote that “there appears to be sufficient NOI to serve the planned debt, but a strategic plan will additionally be needed” for maintenance and improvements. That matches this review at $120 million.

How the review works
  • Two cases for bad years. Milder: 30% of revenue doesn't change with visits, and 40% of operating costs fall with visits, so each 10% drop in visits cuts earnings about $2 million. Harsher, based on Karen Blakemore's ChatGPT analysis: revenue falls 80% as fast as visits and costs fall little, about $3.2 million per 10% drop.
  • Ikon. Sources told The Colorado Sun that 60% to 75% of Eldora's lift-ticket revenue comes from Alterra's Ikon payments (Colorado Sun); the purchase agreement describes that money as payable “for scans” (APA §2.2, §8.13). The review assumes the Ikon agreement keeps its current terms. The current agreement is among POWDR, Snowbird Resort, and Alterra, so the Town would need its own agreement for Eldora (APA definitions, §3.2(f)).
  • Bad years aren't far-fetched. Colorado Ski Country USA's preliminary figures put statewide skier visits at 10.5 million in 2025–26, down 24% from 13.9 million, in a low-snow winter. SAM also noted that advance-purchase season passes kept revenue at many resorts from falling as much as visits (SAM). Eldora's own numbers aren't public.
  • Bonds. The Town now models rates “closer to 8.5 percent.” Real bonds wouldn't have equal payments; the January plan included bonds with later payments, which ease early years but cost more later. The bonds would be repaid only from the resort; if the reserve ran out, remedies would be the bondholders' (where things stand). The bond payment schedules released so far show higher payments than the level $10.6 million a year this review assumes for $120 million, and don't show the bond amounts behind them (above).
  • The price. The price is the borrowing less the other costs paid from the bonds. The draft bond documents would also fund a CapEx fund, a capital maintenance fund, and an operating reserve at closing, with the deposits left blank (draft indenture §3.02, §5.05); the May 2026 draft sets an operating reserve of $11.7 million at closing and a CapEx requirement of $47.5 million. It also measures coverage after administrative costs, including POWDR's transition fees and amounts owed to Alterra, which this review doesn't subtract, so the review may overstate what the resort can borrow. Upkeep is the Town's $2 million a year, which doesn't cover new lifts or lodges. The price is an estimate of what the reported earnings could support, not a Town or POWDR figure, and says nothing about what POWDR would accept.

The records that would settle these questions, including SE Group's report, the revenue forecast behind the bond payment schedules, and the amounts set aside at closing, are on the Eldora Record's list of records anyone can request. This is also why I think the Board should require an independent review that tests the final terms at current interest rates and through bad snow years before any vote on a bond ordinance.

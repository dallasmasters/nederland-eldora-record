Citizen Plan for Nederland's Budget Crisis

Written by Nederland residents Melody Baumhover, Karen Blakemore, Dallas Masters, and Ben White, from the Town's public records. Published Nov. 24, 2026. Not a Town document or legal advice. Amounts are rounded estimates.

A plan to pay for the Eldora acquisition costs and balance the Town's 2027 budget, using the least money from the Town's other funds and paying all of it back.

This plan needs no change to the 2026 budget. No supplemental appropriation, no budget amendment, and no transfer from another fund in 2026. Staff forecast 2026 General Fund spending at $5,493,955, which is $180,221 under the $5,674,176 the Board approved. Why

This plan rebuilds the reserves of the Community Center, Sewer, and Water funds. Everything those funds provide is a loan, repaid with interest on a written schedule, so each fund gets its money back. Under the Town's proposal, about $2.6 million leaves those funds as permanent transfers. How the loans are repaid

1. The plan in brief

The problem

  • The Town has paid or owes about $2.9 million for work on the possible purchase of Eldora Mountain Resort. About $1.25 million is paid, and $1.62 million is owed to seven firms.
  • Staff forecast the General Fund's available balance at −$2.18 million at the end of 2026, and −$2.53 million at the end of 2027 if nothing changes.
  • State law requires the 2027 budget to balance.
  • Staff have proposed covering the deficit with about $2.6 million in transfers from the Water, Sewer, and Community Center funds, while General Fund spending stays about the same.

What this plan does

  1. Stop new spending on the acquisition, and hold the bills. The Town spends no more on the acquisition, because the General Fund has no money for it, and pays no acquisition bill until the Board approves a settlement at a public meeting.
  2. Make no change to the 2026 budget. No supplemental appropriation, no amendment, and no transfer from another fund. Forecast 2026 spending is under the General Fund's approved budget, so none is needed.
  3. Charge each fund its own costs. Move costs that belong to Water, Sewer, and the Community Center out of the General Fund.
  4. Settle the bills for less. Negotiate each bill through counsel. This plan uses a planning figure of about $406,000 in savings.
  5. Reduce the Sheriff contract. Give notice by Dec. 1 to reduce it to a sergeant and two deputies, and ask Boulder County to bill the Town for two of the three positions. Keep the co-responder, a behavioral health responder who works with deputies, and the traffic shifts. This saves about $296,000 in 2027 and $507,000 a year after.
  6. Borrow from the Community Center Fund first. Borrow about $825,000, the part of its balance that voters didn't restrict, and pay it back. The fund has spent more on the center than its sales tax brought in, so on the Town's stated practice of spending restricted money first, the balance is other revenue. Keep the center's operations funded and defer its capital work.
  7. Cut General Fund spending, and ask the Downtown Development Authority to pay downtown costs. The cuts come from a review of the fund's spending and an organizational review. Together these save about $178,000 in 2027. Employees keep the 3% raise, and about $35,000 of the cuts pays for it.
  8. Borrow from Sewer and Water last. At the end of 2027, borrow up to about $400,000 from Sewer, the amount its own draft plan shows it can lend and still keep its operating reserve. Then borrow from Water only the amount still needed, about $523,000. Both funds have made loans by Board resolution before.

All three are loans. This plan asks the Board to borrow from the Community Center Fund, Sewer, and Water by resolution, each with an interest rate and a repayment schedule (loan terms). Sewer and Water have both lent this way before: Water lent the Downtown Development Authority $482,500 in 2012 and $340,000 in 2016, and Sewer lent it $13,000 in 2015. The 2016 audit counted the Water loan as a resource of the borrower.

If a loan can't be counted toward the 2027 budget. The Town's auditor decides that. The Community Center Fund's and Sewer's money is fee money, so each loan could instead be a transfer that the General Fund returns on a written schedule. Water's money is mostly sales tax that voters dedicated to water debt, so it can only be lent. If the auditor won't count a Water loan, the Board has to find about $523,000 elsewhere before it adopts the budget. Details

This plan doesn't decide whether the Town buys Eldora. The acquisition effort can continue under Resolution 2026-31. The plan asks only that the Town spend no new money on it, because the General Fund's balance is already about $2.2 million below zero and it has no money to pay for more work. Resolution 2026-31 allows up to $80,000 of term-sheet work if the Board adopts a supplemental appropriation for it. This plan's figures include none of that. If the Board approves it, the amount needed rises by the same amount, and so does the Water loan.

What the plan achieves

  • The 2027 budget balances. The General Fund's balance is $0 at the end of 2027, up from −$2.18 million.
  • About $1.75 million is borrowed from three funds and repaid. The Town's proposal takes about $2.6 million from the same funds, as permanent transfers.
  • Yearly spending falls by about $667,000, so revenue exceeds spending by about $40,000 to $90,000 each year from 2028 through 2031. Under the Town's proposal the General Fund would fall to about −$2.1 million again by the end of 2031.
  • Larger settlement savings, land sales, and cost sharing by the County would each reduce the Water loan (section 3).

Key dates

  • Dec. 1, 2026The Board holds acquisition payments, sends the Sheriff notice, asks the County to share costs, makes no change to the 2026 budget, and directs staff to prepare a 2027 budget that follows this plan.
  • Dec. 8Public hearing on the 2027 budget, with the Town's proposal and a budget that follows this plan side by side.
  • Dec. 15The Board adopts a balanced 2027 budget and the three loan resolutions, and certifies the mill levy.
  • By Dec. 31The Town and County approve the co-responder's 2027 budget, so the service continues without a break.
  • Jan. to April 2027Settlements with the firms, land sales, and a search for Town Attorney services.
  • June 1, 2027The smaller Sheriff contract takes effect.
  • December 2027The Community Center Fund, Sewer, and Water lend only the amount still needed.
  • 2028 and afterYearly savings cover the yearly shortfall, and the loans are repaid as money allows.

2. Compared with the Town's proposal

Staff's Nov. 16 memo proposes transfers from three funds and says the 2027 budget would "generally maintain existing operating expenditures." This plan calls that the Town's proposal. The Board hasn't adopted it.

Where the two agree

  • The starting figures. This plan uses staff's forecast: −$2.18 million at the end of 2026 and −$2.53 million at the end of 2027.
  • The 2027 budget has to balance, and that requires money from other funds.
  • The Community Center Fund has fee money available. Staff say $800,000. This plan borrows $825,000.
  • Water has money available. Staff say up to $1.4 million. This plan borrows $523,000.
  • Sewer has money available. Staff say up to $900,000. This plan borrows up to $400,000.
  • Each fund should pay its own costs. The Interim Town Manager has said she wants this corrected.
  • The Downtown Development Authority isn't dissolved. Staff left that out of the preliminary budget.

Where they differ

  • Reduce spending before borrowing. The Town's proposal keeps spending about the same. This plan lowers it by about $667,000 a year.
  • Make loans, not transfers. All three funds lend the money and are repaid.
  • Make no change to the 2026 budget. Staff's Nov. 16 memo recommended amending the 2026 budget to record the transfers this year; their Dec. 1 memo no longer does. This plan needs no 2026 supplemental appropriation, amendment, or transfer.
  • Borrow a smaller amount, and borrow it later. This plan borrows about $1.75 million at the end of 2027. The Town's proposal transfers $2.6 million in 2026.

What would lower the Water loan

TBD means to be determined: the amount isn't known yet, so the table counts $0. Each dollar from the items below lowers the Water loan by a dollar.

  • Larger settlement savings than the planning figure.
  • Land sales. Sales of Town lots that are free to sell.
  • More County cost sharing. A County share above half of the co-responder, or a share of the substation and dispatch costs.
  • Costs charged to the right fund, and other downtown costs. The amounts aren't known until staff list them.

What the figures assume

The settlement savings are a planning figure, not an offer. The Sheriff savings assume the County bills the Town as this plan proposes, and the co-responder figure assumes the County pays at least half, as it did for 2026. Staff's balance figures are the available balance, after the emergency reserve that TABOR requires: 3% of operating spending, about $102,000 in staff's 2027 budget. So a balance of $0 keeps that reserve. The plan's spending is lower than staff's, so the reserve it needs is smaller, and it stays below that amount through 2031 even if spending grows 3% a year.

What would raise the Water loan

  • The Community Center money can't be used. Water would have to lend about $1.35 million, nearly the $1.4 million staff gave as the most available.
  • The auditor won't count a Water loan toward the budget. The Board would need about $523,000 from the TBD items and further cuts before adopting the budget, because Water's money can't be transferred.
  • The Sheriff notice goes out after Dec. 1. Each month of delay costs about $42,000 of 2027 savings.

4. The plan, step by step

Each step says what the Board or staff would do. Links lead to the reasons and the records behind it.

Phase 1

By the Dec. 1, 2026, Board meeting

1. Hold acquisition payments and protect the other funds

Adopt a resolution that adds to Resolution 2026-31 and repeals nothing in it. It would:

  • hold payment of every acquisition invoice until the Board approves a settlement at a public meeting;
  • take acquisition invoices off the consent agenda and list them separately as "held pending review";
  • allow no transfer from another fund in 2026;
  • add to the monthly report each fund's cash and the next Water and Sewer loan payments.

2. Dispute the open invoices in writing

Staff confirm that the stop-work letters required by Resolution 2026-31 went out. Each firm then gets a notice that disputes each open invoice by number and amount and says the bills are under review.

3. Send the Sheriff notice

Give the Boulder County Sheriff's Office six months' notice to reduce the contract to a sergeant and two deputies, with no detective or animal control position. Ask the County to bill the Town for two of the three positions, because the deputies also cover the mountain area outside town. Tell the County that the Town can't afford the Sheriff contract or the co-responder agreement at their current cost. The evidence

4. Keep the co-responder and ask the County to share costs

This plan keeps the co-responder. Approve its 2027 budget before Dec. 31, with the County paying at least half, as it did for 2026. The agreement requires an approved budget for each year, so approving it by then keeps the service running. Ask the County to share the substation rent and to explain the dispatch fee. Details

5. Ask counsel and finance staff for written answers before Dec. 15

The Town Attorney answers the legal questions the budget depends on. The Town Manager and finance staff report in writing on each loan: what it does to the lending fund's reserve, capital work, and debt payments, and how the General Fund would repay it. The Town Attorney's firm is one of the seven firms owed money, so the Town hires temporary outside counsel for the matters where the firm has a conflict of interest. No step in this plan waits for a legal opinion. Each rests on records the Town already has. The Town Attorney should read the state water and sewer loan agreements before the Sewer and Water loans are made at the end of 2027. The questions

6. Freeze discretionary spending for the rest of 2026

The saving is small, about $20,000 to $50,000, but it helps keep 2026 spending below the amount the Board approved. Details

7. Start charging each fund its own costs

Staff list each cost now charged to the General Fund that belongs to another fund, with the reason for the charge and the amount for 2026 and 2027. Details

8. Take the steps staff can take without a Board vote

  • Keep all acquisition records and suspend routine deletion.
  • Notify the Town's insurer of possible claims by the firms.
  • Confirm that cash is available for the next state water and sewer loan payments, about $428,000 a year.
  • Ask each firm to confirm in writing that no retainer or monthly fee accrues while work is stopped. This doesn't end any agreement.
  • Ask the state Division of Local Government for free technical help on the 2027 budget.
  • Have the Town Attorney check the expired purchase agreement and term sheet for any expense reimbursement or deposit owed to the Town.
  • Order appraisals for Town land that is free to sell.
  • Give the Board the figures listed below.

9. Fill the open Board seat as soon as possible

The Town is taking applications through Dec. 28. State law sets no deadline for an appointment, and the Town set the application period itself. The Board can amend its announcement to say it will appoint as soon as possible, so that a full Board votes on the 2027 budget.

10. Direct staff to prepare a 2027 budget that follows this plan

For the Board and the public to compare the two budgets at the Dec. 8 hearing, staff need the Board's direction on Dec. 1. The Board directs staff to bring a 2027 budget with this plan's changes to the hearing, next to the Town's own proposal, and to report what Water's and Sewer's cash is made of and which costs belong to other funds. The specific budget changes are in a list of 2027 budget requests (PDF).

11. Ask the Downtown Development Authority to put the Visitor Center on its next agenda

An agreement for the Authority to take on the Visitor Center needs its board's approval at a public meeting. Starting now allows the agreement to be ready for the 2027 budget. Details

Phase 2

Dec. 8 hearing and Dec. 15 adoption: the 2027 budget

1. Adopt a balanced 2027 budget built from the sources in section 3

The budget would include:

  • The Sheriff contract at the reduced level from June 1, the co-responder, and the traffic shifts.
  • The 3% raise for employees, and General Fund cuts of $151,000, from a review of the fund's spending and an organizational review. Details
  • A Community Center Fund loan of up to $825,000, made at year-end, with the center's operations fully funded. Conditions
  • A Sewer loan of up to $400,000, made at year-end. Conditions
  • A Water loan of "up to" the amount still needed, made at year-end. Conditions
  • A line for land-sale money, which can be spent only after a sale is completed. Details
  • An agreement with the Downtown Development Authority to take on the Visitor Center. Details

The Board adopts the three loan resolutions with the budget. Each resolution authorizes a loan of up to the stated amount, sets the interest rate, the repayment schedule, and the conditions, and says the money moves at the end of 2027, only for the amount still needed. The money doesn't have to move before the budget is adopted. A budget is a plan for the year, and it has to show enough resources for the year as a whole. Why

2. Add a separate line for acquisition settlements

It can be spent only on a settlement the Board approves at a public meeting.

3. Put in writing any cash the General Fund has used from other funds

The Town's funds share one cash account. When the General Fund pays bills with a negative balance, it uses other funds' cash. Once staff report how much of the shared cash belongs to each fund, the Board should record any amount the General Fund has used as a loan with repayment terms. If it isn't recorded, the auditor may report it as a transfer the Board never voted on.

4. Hold the hearing, adopt the budget, and file it with the state

Consider every written objection residents file before adoption, as state law requires (C.R.S. 29-1-107 and 29-1-108). Certify the mill levy by Dec. 15. File the budget with the state by Jan. 31. Brief the new auditor on how the acquisition costs and the cash owed between funds will be reported.

7. Questions and answers

Doesn't a negative balance at the end of 2026 break the law?

No. The budget law limits what a budget may provide for, and 2026 spending is forecast below the amount the Board approved. The 2027 budget is the one that has to cover the negative balance. Expect the 2026 audit to report the negative balance whichever approach the Board takes. Details

Does the 2026 budget need a supplemental appropriation or an amendment?

No. Staff forecast 2026 General Fund spending at $5,493,955, which is $180,221 less than the Board approved. Details

Does this plan end the Eldora purchase?

No. The plan doesn't decide that. Resolution 2026-31 still controls. The plan asks only that the Town spend no new money on the acquisition, because the General Fund's balance is already about $2.2 million below zero and it has no money to pay for more work.

Why not adopt the Town's proposed transfers?

They are permanent, they come in 2026 before any cut can reduce them, and spending stays about the same, so by the end of 2031 the General Fund would be about $2.1 million below zero again. They are also hard to return. Water and Sewer are TABOR enterprises. If money is transferred out and the General Fund later sends it back, the money sent back could count as a grant to the enterprise, and grants are limited to 10% of each fund's yearly revenue, about $100,000 for Water and $120,000 for Sewer. A loan is different: repaying a loan isn't a grant, so that limit doesn't apply. Comparison

Can a loan count toward a balanced budget?

That isn't settled. Staff say a loan between funds doesn't count. The Town's 2016 audit counted a Water loan as a resource of the fund that borrowed it. The Town's auditor decides, and the plan asks the Board to put the question in writing now, along with whether a loan the Board has approved but not yet made can be budgeted. If the answer is no, the Community Center Fund's and Sewer's loans can be made as transfers that are returned, and the Board has to find about $523,000 in place of the Water loan. Details

Do the loans have to be made before the 2027 budget is adopted?

No. The budget law says a budget may not provide for spending above the revenue and balances available for the year. It is a plan for the whole year, so money in it doesn't have to be in hand on the day it is adopted. The 2027 budget would show the three loans as money coming in, and the Board would adopt the loan resolutions the same night, so the loans are a commitment and not only a budget line. The money moves at the end of 2027, so that settlements and land sales reduce the amount first. Until then the General Fund's bills are paid from the Town's shared cash account, as they are now. Whether a loan that is approved but not yet made can be budgeted is part of the written question for the auditor. Details

Is the Community Center Fund's money restricted?

Voters restricted the fund's sales tax. Nothing found restricts its fees, rentals, or interest. The audit says the Town spends restricted money first, and the fund has spent more on the center than the sales tax brought in. The audits also label the whole balance restricted, so the auditor confirms the amount before the loan is made. Details

Why borrow from Sewer before Water?

Sewer's fees cover its operating costs, and its sales tax all goes to its debt payments, so the money it has beyond its costs is fee money. Water's fees are below its operating costs, and most of its cash is sales tax that voters dedicated to water debt. That money can be lent, but not transferred. Sewer Water

Why borrow only $400,000 from Sewer, when staff say up to $900,000 is available?

The $400,000 is the amount Sewer's own draft plan shows it can lend and still keep its operating reserve in every year through 2034. Sewer's debt coverage is close to the minimum its loans require, and about $2.5 million of sewer work is planned for 2033. Details

How do the loans get repaid?

Under the plan, revenue exceeds spending by about $40,000 to $90,000 each year from 2028. Repaying $1.75 million over 10 years takes about $175,000 a year. The rest would come from land sales, larger settlement savings, costs charged to the right fund, payments by the Downtown Development Authority, more County cost sharing, and a review of fees and other revenue. The amount from each is TBD. Details

What if the County doesn't agree to bill for two positions?

The Town's position is strong. Deputies assigned to the Town spend about half of their uncommitted patrol time in town and also cover the area outside it, yet the Town pays their full cost and the substation rent. About a third of the co-responder team's 2025 contacts were outside town, and residents also pay about $1.79 million a year in County taxes. The notice to reduce the contract to a sergeant and two deputies doesn't depend on the County's answer, so it can go out on Dec. 1. The evidence

Is a smaller Sheriff contract safe?

The Town would still have a sergeant and two deputies assigned to it. In the hours those positions don't cover, the Town would receive the service the Sheriff's Office already provides to the unincorporated areas next to it, such as Ridge Road and Magnolia Road. The Board should ask the Sheriff's Office in writing what hours the three positions would cover. The reduction takes six months to take effect, so there is time to get answers. Details

What if a firm sues over a held bill?

The Town tells each firm in writing that its bills are under review, which is accurate. Outside counsel handles the disputes, because the Town Attorney's firm is one of the seven firms owed. Staff notify the Town's insurer now. Details

What are the General Fund cuts?

$151,000 in 2027, from a review of every General Fund department and line and an organizational review. About $35,000 of that pays for the 3% raise for employees, which the plan keeps. $63,000 is listed: Board goodwill, travel and training, dues, and professional services. Details

Terms used in this plan

General Fund
The Town's main fund. It pays for administration, the Sheriff contract, parks, and most other services.
Loan
Money one Town fund lends another by Board resolution, with an interest rate and a schedule for paying it back.
Transfer
Money moved from one fund to another with no requirement to pay it back.
Fee money
Money a fund collects in fees, rentals, and service charges. It is different from sales tax that voters set aside for one purpose.
Dedicated sales tax
A share of the sales tax that voters approved for a stated purpose, such as paying water debt.
Supplemental appropriation
A Board vote during the year to spend more than the budget it approved.
TBD
To be determined. The amount isn't known yet, so the plan counts $0.
Planning figure
An amount used to make the budget add up. It is not an offer or a prediction.
Co-responder
A behavioral health responder who works with deputies, under a separate agreement with Boulder County.
TABOR enterprise
Under Colorado's Taxpayer's Bill of Rights, a government-owned business, such as a water utility, that can issue its own bonds and gets less than 10% of its yearly revenue in grants from state and local governments.
Tap fees
One-time fees paid when a property connects to the water or sewer system.
Operating reserve
Cash a fund keeps on hand to cover its operating costs.
Debt coverage
A fund's yearly revenue left after operating costs, divided by its yearly debt payments. The state loans to Water and Sewer require at least 1.10.
Net position
What a fund owns minus what it owes. The audit labels part of it "unrestricted."
Warrant list
The list of payments the Board approves at each meeting.
Consent agenda
Routine items the Board approves in one vote without discussion.
Mill levy
The property tax rate, which the Board certifies each December.

What the budget law requires

  • The 2027 budget must balance. No budget "shall provide for expenditures in excess of available revenues and beginning fund balances" (C.R.S. 29-1-103(2)). With a beginning balance of about −$2.18 million, 2027 resources must exceed 2027 spending by that much.
  • Ending 2026 with a negative balance is not itself a violation. The auditor will likely report it. A deficit that is reported openly, with a plan to eliminate it, is better than a rushed transfer.
  • Cuts, settlement savings, land sales, and transfers count toward balancing.
  • Loans between funds are this plan's default. The Town has made them before. The Water and Sewer funds lent the Downtown Development Authority $842,500 from 2012 through 2016. The later ones were approved by Board resolution with an interest rate, a deadline, and a finding that the lending fund could provide the money "without hampering the operations or replacement schedules" (Resolutions 2014-33, 2015-06, and 2016-20).
  • Whether a loan counts toward balancing isn't settled. The usual accounting treatment, and staff's Oct. 30 analysis, is that a loan between funds adds cash and an equal amount owed, so the General Fund's balance doesn't rise. But the 2016 audit reported Water's $340,000 loan in the Downtown Development Authority's fund as "Proceeds from Issuance of Debt," which raised that fund's balance, and the auditor gave a clean opinion. The Town's auditor decides.
  • If a loan can't be counted. The Community Center Fund's and Sewer's loans can each be made as a transfer instead, with a Board resolution that sets a written schedule for returning it, with each payment subject to annual appropriation. A transfer counts toward balancing. Water's money can't be transferred (Water), so the Board would have to find that amount, about $523,000, elsewhere before adopting the budget.

Why the 2026 budget needs no supplemental appropriation or transfer

Staff's Nov. 16 memo recommends recording the transfers in an amended 2026 budget, "rather than carrying that deficit forward into FY27." This plan doesn't, for three reasons. Staff's Dec. 1 memo says they won't present an amended 2026 budget, because "the General Fund is likely not to exceed the budgeted 2026 appropriations," and are still evaluating the legal and audit considerations.

  • A 2026 budget change isn't required. Staff forecast 2026 General Fund spending, including the acquisition costs, at $5,493,955. The Board approved $5,674,176 (Resolution 2025-28). Spending under the approved amount needs no supplemental appropriation, amendment, or transfer.
  • Resolution 2026-31 doesn't require one either. It asks staff to present a transfer, supplemental appropriation, or revised appropriation for the 2026 acquisition costs. It doesn't require the Board to adopt one.
  • A 2026 transfer would take the most money. A 2026 transfer must cover the whole deficit, about $2.0 to $2.2 million, before any 2027 cut, settlement, or land sale can reduce it.
  • A transfer is hard to reverse. If money is transferred out of Water and the General Fund later sends it back, the money sent back could count as a grant, which is limited to about $100,000 a year. Repaying a loan isn't a grant, so a loan doesn't have that problem (see Water).
  • What could change the 2026 forecast. The $180,221 is an estimate of how far spending will be below the approved amount. If large bills arrive in December, or the Town spends more on the acquisition, spending could reach that amount. The term-sheet work that Resolution 2026-31 allows would need a supplemental appropriation, which is one reason this plan asks for no new acquisition spending.

The Community Center Fund loan

Why it comes before Water. The fund has no state loan and isn't a TABOR enterprise, so repaying it raises none of the questions that Water does.

What voters restricted. The 2002 and 2022 ballot questions restrict the Community Center's sales tax. No ordinance, ballot question, or bond document found restricts the fund's fees, rentals, or interest. From 2016 through 2025 the fund spent $4,139,743 on the Community Center and took in $3,479,962 of sales tax, and the 2025 audit says the Town's practice is "to use restricted resources first." On that practice, the sales tax has all been spent on the center, and the balance is other revenue.

How the audits label the balance. The audits label the whole balance "Restricted for Community Center." Under the Town's stated practice, that balance isn't unspent sales tax. The Town's auditor confirms the amount when the loan is made.

The Sheriff contract and County costs

What the plan proposes. A sergeant and two deputies, with no half-time detective or animal control position, billed to the Town as two positions. At 2026 rates that lowers the contract from $931,629 to about $424,400.

That saves about $296,000 in 2027, from June 1, and about $507,000 a year after.

Evidence that the Town pays for policing outside its limits.

  • Under the contract's Second Amendment, the day deputy and the night deputy each spend about half of their uncommitted patrol time in town. The base agreement makes them responsible for calls in the unincorporated mountain area. The Town pays the full cost of each position.
  • The deputies work from a substation the Town pays for, with $21,660 budgeted for rent.
  • About a third of the mountain co-responder team's 2025 contacts were outside Nederland.
  • Property owners and shoppers in town pay about $1.79 million a year in County property and sales taxes. In proportion to the County's budget, about $208,000 of the property tax supports the Sheriff's Office, before the contract.

The notice period is six months. The Second Amendment (Resolution 2025-04) says a reduction of positions takes effect six months after written notice. A Sept. 15, 2026, staff memo refers to one year, which was the earlier term.

What three positions would cover. Three positions can't staff every shift. In the other hours the Town would receive the service the Sheriff's Office provides to the unincorporated areas next to it, such as Ridge Road and Magnolia Road. The Sheriff has a countywide duty to keep the peace (C.R.S. 30-10-516), and in January 2023, when the Town had no marshal, the Town reported that the Sheriff's Office was responding to all priority 1 calls and providing nighttime coverage. The Board should ask the Sheriff's Office in writing what hours the positions would cover and what service continues in the other hours.

Keep the traffic shifts. They cost $124,000 and are budgeted to bring in $230,000 in fines.

Keep the co-responder. This plan asks the Board to approve its 2027 budget before Dec. 31, so the service continues. It has its own agreement with Boulder County Community Services, which says "no services shall be provided, and no payment shall be due, for any contract year for which a budget has not been approved." The full cost is $130,461 a year. The County paid half of 2026, and this plan assumes it pays at least half in 2027. The lodging tax, about $45,000 to $53,000 a year, could pay part of it, because voters approved that tax for health and human services and law enforcement.

Other County costs. The Town pays $56,514 for dispatch under a separate agreement and budgets $21,660 for the substation's rent. This plan asks the County to share the cost of the co-responder and the substation and to explain how the dispatch fee is set. The amount is TBD.

The Sewer loan

The plan borrows from Sewer. It doesn't transfer Sewer's money. The Sewer Fund has lent before: $13,000 to the Downtown Development Authority in 2015, by Board resolution, with an interest rate, a deadline, and a finding that the fund could provide the money "without hampering the operations or replacement schedules" (Resolution 2015-06). A loan is also better for Sewer than a transfer, because repaying a loan isn't limited by TABOR.

Why Sewer comes before Water. The money Sewer has beyond its costs is fee money. Water's is mostly dedicated sales tax. The 2026 budget shows Sewer's charges for services above its operating costs in each year from 2023 through 2026, by about $119,000 to $192,000 a year. Sewer's dedicated sales tax, $1,090,638 over those years, was less than its debt payments of $1,144,847, so none of that tax remains after the debt payments. The 2025 audit labels $839,238 of Sewer's net position unrestricted.

The amount. Up to about $400,000. The Town's draft 10-year utility plan shows Sewer's cash staying at least $399,409 above its operating reserve in every year through 2034. Staff's memo put the amount available at up to $900,000.

Conditions.

  • It is budgeted "up to" a stated amount and made at year-end only for the amount the earlier sources don't cover.
  • Sewer stays above its operating reserve and its required debt coverage in every year of its plan, and no capital work or debt payment is delayed.
  • It is approved by resolution as a loan, with an interest rate and a repayment schedule. Only if the Town's auditor won't count a loan toward the budget does it become a transfer, with a written schedule for returning it.
  • Before the loan is made, the Town Attorney reads the state sewer loan agreements and the 2018 debt vote and reports anything that limits it.

Limits on the Sewer loan. Sewer's debt coverage is close to the minimum: 1.12 in 2026, and its loans require 1.10. About $2.5 million of sewer work is planned for 2033. Sewer is a TABOR enterprise, so if a transfer were used instead of a loan, the money returned to Sewer would be limited to about $120,000 a year. Tap fees (plant investment fees) were budgeted at $136,235 a year for 2025 and 2026. Sewer received $26,976 in 2023, $7,608 in 2024, $114,780 in 2025, and $8,008 through September 2026. In October, the Board let TEENS pay $361,582 of water and sewer tap fees over five years instead of at once (Resolution 2026-28). Staff count tap fees toward Sewer's debt coverage, so staff should confirm the tap fees expected in 2026 and 2027 and how much fee money Sewer holds.

The Water loan

Water's money can be lent, but not transferred. Most of Water's cash is sales tax that voters dedicated to water debt, and Town Code Sec. 4-58 bars changing the use of that tax without a vote. A transfer would change its use. A loan doesn't, because the money is repaid to Water. The precedent is Water's own loans to the Downtown Development Authority, $482,500 in 2012 and $340,000 in 2016, made by Board resolution after voters dedicated the tax, and repaid.

What Water's money is. Staff's memo says the transfers would use user fees. The 2026 budget shows Water's user fees below its operating costs in each year from 2023 through 2026, by about $43,000 to $76,000 a year. Over the same years Water received $1,090,638 of dedicated sales tax and paid $573,033 on its debt. So Water's cash is mostly sales tax left after debt payments, tap fees, and older savings, not fee surplus. The 2025 audit labels $1,958,661 of Water's net position unrestricted. This is why Water comes last, why the amount should be as small as possible, and why the money has to be repaid.

Conditions.

  • It is budgeted "up to" a stated amount and made at year-end only for the amount the other sources don't cover.
  • It doesn't delay Water's capital plan or debt payments.
  • It is approved by resolution, with an interest rate, a repayment schedule, and a yearly public report.
  • Before the loan is made, the Town Attorney reads the state water loan agreements and the 1998 and 2012 sales tax votes and reports anything that limits the loan.

If the loan can't be counted toward the budget. The 2016 audit reported Water's $340,000 loan as a resource of the fund that borrowed it. Staff's Oct. 30 analysis says a loan to the General Fund wouldn't count. The Town's auditor decides, and the Board should ask in writing now, with the 2016 audit pages attached. If the answer is no, Water's $523,000 has to come from somewhere else: larger settlement savings, land sales, a larger Community Center Fund loan, more County cost sharing, and more cuts.

The TABOR limit on grants. Water is a TABOR enterprise, and it loses that status if grants reach 10% of its yearly revenue, about $100,000. Repaying a loan isn't a grant.

Why Water is last. Its fees don't cover its operations, most of its cash is dedicated sales tax, and it can only lend.

What the Town Attorney should check. The state loans are secured by utility revenues and sales tax. Voters dedicated sales tax to utility debt in 1998 and 2012, and Ordinance 710 (2012) says "to amend the purposes for which tax revenue is used, voter approval is required."

General Fund cuts

The plan counts $151,000 of General Fund cuts in 2027 and $168,000 a year after. The Board and staff would reach that through a review of all General Fund spending and an organizational review.

The spending freeze for the rest of 2026. About $33,000 remained in discretionary lines by the Nov. 9 warrant list, and another $47,000 was budgeted in lines with no payments shown. A realistic 2026 saving is $20,000 to $50,000. The Big Springs grant match ($38,244) should not be frozen, because cutting it could cause the Town to lose the grant.

Lines the review should start with, $63,000:

  • Board goodwill, $10,000;
  • travel, training, lodging, and meals in all departments, up to $29,000;
  • memberships and dues, up to $11,600;
  • administrative professional services, $11,600.

Smaller budget lines. A line-by-line review found up to about $47,000 more in smaller lines; and substation rent, up to $21,660, if the County shares it.

The 3% raise. The plan keeps the 3% raise for employees. Staff's tables put its cost to the General Fund at about $35,000 in 2027: the available balance at the end of 2027 is −$2,563,003 with the raise and −$2,528,102 without it. The plan pays for the raise with General Fund cuts of the same amount, so the raise adds nothing to the loans.

The rest of the review, $88,000 in 2027 and $105,000 a year after. The review covers every General Fund department and line, including the smaller lines listed above. It includes an organizational review: how the Town's work is organized, and which fund each part of it belongs to. Its changes take effect by April 1. A saving found in the Community Center Fund or a utility fund stays in that fund.

One budget line staff should explain. Administrative professional services was budgeted at $11,600 for 2026, and about $101,600 had been charged to it by Nov. 9, mostly monthly invoices from P&M Holding Group. Staff should say what the work is and when it ends.

Charging each fund its own costs

  • The Interim Town Manager has said she wants costs that are charged to the General Fund but belong to other funds corrected. Each cost moved lowers the deficit by the same amount.
  • This is a correction, not a transfer. The other fund pays for a service it received, so nothing is repaid later.
  • Each charge needs a documented reason, such as staff time, billing, insurance, or audit work. A charge with no documented reason is in effect a transfer.
  • The current charges rose by the same percentage for every fund in 2025 and 2026, and the Budget Book shows no calculation behind them. Staff should explain how they are set.
  • The acquisition costs can't be moved this way. The other funds didn't receive those services.
  • If a 2026 amendment combines corrections with transfers, the Board should vote on them separately.
  • This plan assumes the amount is small, because Water and Sewer already pay the General Fund about $200,000 a year for administration.

Settling with the firms

  • The Town disputes each open invoice in writing and says the bills are under review.
  • Outside counsel negotiates, because the Town Attorney's firm is one of the firms owed.
  • Each settlement is a new agreement, approved at a public meeting, with the money approved before signing.
  • Payments after 2027 are subject to each year's budget.
  • The $406,000 in this plan is a planning figure. The amounts are set in negotiation.

The Downtown Development Authority

  • The Visitor Center. It costs $44,921 against $17,950 of revenue in the 2026 budget, a net cost of about $27,000. The budget says the Board may evaluate "lease or partnership options for the space."
  • Other downtown costs. The Authority may be able to pay General Fund costs inside its district and within its plan, such as the public restrooms, street maintenance, and snow removal. Each needs the Authority board's approval at a public meeting. The amount is TBD.
  • The Authority has money available. Its fund balance was $1,695,723 at the end of 2025, restricted to downtown development, and it takes in about $400,000 a year.
  • What the Authority can't pay for. A loan or general transfer to cover the deficit is likely outside its purposes.

Selling Town land

  • Budget only land that is free to sell, with no grant or easement limits. Count it only after a sale is completed.
  • Money from land sales goes to closing the 2027 shortfall and rebuilding reserves, never to operating costs.
  • Resolution 2011-30 requires an appraisal less than three months old at the time of sale.
  • Some land may earn more leased than sold.

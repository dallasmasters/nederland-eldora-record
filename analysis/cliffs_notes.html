The Eldora Deal Cliffs Notes

Dallas Masters, Nederland resident · Nov. 12, 2026 · Figures through the Nov. 9, 2026, warrant list, updated with the record, last Nov. 27, 2026

Nederland has spent two years trying to buy Eldora Mountain Resort. The purchase hasn't happened, but the Town has run up about $2.9 million in bills trying to make it happen. If the deal never closes, the Town has to cover that itself. That is nearly as much as the General Fund spends on everything else in a year.

This page tells the story in order, in plain language. Chapters 1 through 6 retell what the Eldora Record shows, and each ends with links to the part of the record that has the details and sources; if the two ever differ, the record is correct. Passages marked “My reading” and all of chapter 7 are my opinion. For facts only, see the Eldora Record; my fuller opinions are on a separate page.

1.The plan to buy Eldora

In 2024, POWDR, the company that owns Eldora, put the ski area up for sale, and the Town decided to try to buy it. The Board began discussing it in closed session on Sept. 3, 2024. Its first open discussion was on Nov. 19, 2024.

The Town gave two main reasons: local control of the mountain, and long-term revenue, including “addressing recurring budget shortfalls through a more stable revenue base.”

  • A new Town business. The resort would be owned by the Mountain Recreation Enterprise, a Town-owned business set up for this purpose.
  • Borrowing paid back by the resort. The Town would sell revenue bonds, which are loans from investors. Lift tickets, passes, and other resort income would repay them. Property taxes and the Town's regular budget would not.
  • Big numbers. In January 2026, the Board approved a $120 million purchase price and up to $225 million in bonds.

Think of it like buying a rental house with a loan that only the rent can repay. If the rent falls short, the lender takes the house, not your paycheck. But you still have to pay the lawyers and inspectors you hire before closing, even if you never get the keys. That is where Nederland's money is at risk now.

2.The lawyers and consultants the Town hired

Starting in January 2025, the Town hired real estate lawyers, bond lawyers, water-rights lawyers, accountants to check the resort's books, ski-industry consultants, surveyors, and a financial advisor. The resolutions that let staff hire them set no dollar limits.

Some firms get paid only if the sale closes. Others get paid either way:

The purchase agreement also said each side pays its own costs, whether or not the sale closes. The Town planned to pay itself back from the bond money at closing. Until then, it pays the bills itself.

4.How the Town recorded the costs

At first, the Town didn't treat the Eldora bills as spending. Through October 2026, it recorded them in a General Fund account called “Due From,” as money it expected to be paid back from the bonds. Accountants call this a receivable.

  • The costs weren't in the budget. Neither the 2025 nor the 2026 budget includes them. The Board approved the payments on the regular bill lists (“warrant lists”), each approved with the rest of the consent agenda.
  • The General Fund's balance looked better than its cash. The General Fund pays for the Town's day-to-day work: administration, police, streets, parks, and the like. At the end of 2025 it showed a balance of about $1 million, a figure that counted the 2025 Eldora costs as money owed to it. Its cash was negative by $757,482.
  • Other funds' cash covered the gap. The Town keeps all its funds' cash in one bank pool and tracks each fund's share on paper. When the General Fund's share runs out, its bills are paid with cash that belongs to the Water Fund, the Sewer Fund, and others.

That way of recording the costs only works if bonds are sold, and right now there is no purchase agreement and no authority to sell bonds.

On Nov. 9, 2026, the Town changed course. New Eldora bills started going to a General Fund account called “Eldora Expense,” with a $0 budget. The same day, its budget forecast counted its whole $2.76 million estimate as General Fund spending, and said the 2025 payments would be moved out of receivables.

My reading
Counting the costs as spending is right. It means they now have to be covered in the budget.

5.What the law allows

State law, the Colorado Constitution, and taxes voters approved for specific uses all limit how the Town can pay these costs.

  • Spending needs Board approval in the budget. Colorado law says a town can't spend or contract to spend more than its budget authorizes (“appropriates”) for each fund. The 2026 General Fund authority is $5,674,176, a limit for the fund as a whole. Staff forecast 2026 spending, including the Eldora costs, at $5,493,955, about $180,000 under it. The room is mostly there because grant projects budgeted at $2,250,000 largely didn't happen this year, so their grant money didn't come either. That keeps 2026 within its limit, but it doesn't pay for the Eldora costs; it's why the General Fund ends the year below zero.
  • A budget can't spend more than the Town has. Spending can't be more than expected revenue plus money left over from past years. A fund that starts the year below zero has to make up the difference with more revenue, less spending, or a transfer from another fund. Staff's analysis is that a loan from another fund doesn't count, because under standard accounting rules a loan adds an equal debt. But the Town's 2016 audit counted a Water Fund loan as a resource of the fund that borrowed it, so the question isn't settled.
  • TABOR requires a public vote for any new tax or tax increase, and for debt or other financial obligations lasting more than a year that aren't backed by cash set aside in advance. TABOR's emergency reserve, $147,000, can't be used for a revenue shortfall.
  • Some money can be used only for certain purposes. The Community Center Fund ($890,426 on Sept. 30, 2026) comes mostly from a sales tax voters approved for community-center purposes, most recently in April 2022, and the Town's own books list all of it as restricted. The Downtown Development Authority's money ($1,695,723) is mostly property tax collected for downtown projects and debts, from every taxing district's levy, not just the Town's. Moving either into the General Fund doesn't change what it can be spent on.
  • Water and sewer money comes from rates and a sales tax voters dedicated to their debt, and repays their state loans. Staff now propose a one-way transfer from their rate money, which voters didn't restrict, rather than a loan; staff dropped the loan because it wouldn't improve the General Fund's balance. Whether a transfer is allowed is a question for the Town attorney.
  • Bankruptcy and dissolving the Town aren't ways out. The record found no Colorado law that lets a town file for bankruptcy, and a town can dissolve only after providing for all its debts.

In short, the General Fund will have to pay the $2.9 million, most likely over several years.

6.If the deal closes

If the deal closes, bond money would repay the Town for most of the $2.9 million. But owning a ski area has its own risks.

  • The debt would be large. The Board authorized up to $225 million in bonds, and an earlier plan expected about $180 million. The Town now models interest rates near 8.5%. The restructured deal is meant to borrow less, but no new amount has been made public.
  • Only the resort pays it back. The bonds would be repaid from resort income alone, not from taxes. That protects the Town's budget from the debt itself, but a run of poor snow years could leave too little to pay bondholders.
  • The resort could change hands if the bonds default. Under the sale-leaseback, a bond trustee would hold title and lease the resort back to the Town's business. If the bonds defaulted, the trustee could act “without first completing a traditional foreclosure process,” which could change who runs the resort.
  • The Town might not see profits for years. Under the May 2026 draft bond documents, an outside consultant would approve the resort's budgets, and money would reach the Town only after the resort earned twice its main bond payments for three years in a row.
  • Other local districts would lose tax revenue. Town-owned property is generally tax-exempt. By the record's estimate, the Nederland Fire Protection District would lose $97,000 to $107,000 a year and the library district $29,000 to $32,000.

My reading
My rough review of the resort's reported earnings suggests it can support about $90 million to $95 million in borrowing. That points to a cash price of about $70 million or less, well below $120 million. The Town hasn't named where the difference would come from.

7.What I think will happen if the deal fails

Prediction

I think the deal is more likely to fail than to close. The main reasons are a gap between the $120 million price and what the resort's earnings can support, harder financing at today's interest rates, staff turnover, and the need to redo every major document. Nothing stops POWDR from selling to someone else.

If it fails, here is the sequence I expect. Steps 4 through 8 are what happens if the Board adopts the Citizen Plan, which I wrote with other residents.

  1. The effort winds down slowly. Resolution 2026-31, adopted Nov. 17, limits new spending and requires monthly cost reports. Work continues into 2027, adding modest costs each month, until a deadline passes, financing can't be found, or POWDR moves on.
  2. The final bills arrive. The real estate lawyers bill when the deal ends: about $1.2 million, perhaps less a 10% discount their agreement offers for a failed deal. The bond lawyers bill their hours. Firms that haven't confirmed what they're owed may send more. The total lands around $2.5 million to $3 million.
  3. The costs land in the budget. The Town's forecast already counts its $2.76 million estimate as spending, and nearly all of what the firms confirmed they're owed is in it. Staff's Nov. 16 tables have the General Fund ending 2026 about $2.2 million below zero, counting only money it can spend, and 2027 about $2.5 million below zero, in a fund that spends about $3.2 million a year on operations. The costs fit within 2026's spending limit, so 2026 needs no budget change, but the 2027 budget has to cover the gap.
  4. The Board adopts the Citizen Plan. It holds the acquisition bills and makes no change to the 2026 budget. Forecast 2026 spending is under the amount the Board approved, so 2026 needs no amendment and no transfer. The Town pays no acquisition bill until the Board approves a settlement at a public meeting. All of the Town's funds share one cash account, so until then the General Fund's bills are paid with cash that belongs to the other funds.
  5. Spending comes down first. The Board gives notice to reduce the Sheriff's contract, about $930,000 a year, to a sergeant and two deputies, and asks the County to bill the Town for two of those positions, because deputies also cover the area outside town. That saves about $296,000 in 2027 and $507,000 a year after. A review of the General Fund's spending and an organizational review find about $151,000 of cuts, with the 3% raise for employees kept, and the Downtown Development Authority takes on the Visitor Center. The Town settles the firms' bills for less through counsel.
  6. The 2027 budget balances with loans, not transfers. For the amount the savings don't cover, the General Fund borrows from the Community Center Fund first, then up to about $400,000 from Sewer, then from Water last: about $1.75 million in all, against the $2.6 million staff propose to transfer. The Community Center Fund's and Sewer's spare money is fee money. Water's is mostly sales tax voters dedicated to water debt, because the 2026 budget shows Water's rates below its operating costs in each year since 2023, so I think it can be lent but not transferred. Staff say a loan doesn't count toward a balanced budget. The Town's 2016 audit counted one, so the plan asks the Town's auditor to answer in writing.
  7. During 2027, settlements and land sales lower the amount borrowed. The loans are made at the end of 2027, only for the amount still needed. Each dollar saved on a settlement, and each sale of Town land that is free to sell, lowers the Water loan first. Park and government land needs voter approval to sell, and sales are slow, so the plan counts no land money until a sale is completed.
  8. From 2028, the savings repay the loans. With the plan's changes the General Fund spends about $667,000 a year less, so its revenue exceeds its spending by about $40,000 to $90,000 a year. Repaying $1.75 million over 10 years takes about $175,000 a year, so the rest has to come from land sales, larger settlement savings, costs charged to the right fund, and more County cost sharing. The three funds get their money back, and their reserves are rebuilt. Expect tight budgets and little money for anything new for years.

If the Board adopts the Town's proposal instead: about $2.6 million moves from the Community Center, Water, and Sewer Funds to the General Fund in 2026 as permanent transfers, and spending stays about the same. The General Fund would then spend about $500,000 a year more than it takes in, and would be about $2.1 million below zero again by the end of 2031. The three funds would not get the money back, and water and sewer rates may rise to rebuild their reserves.

What doesn't happen: the Town doesn't go bankrupt or dissolve, because Colorado law allows neither as a way out of debts. Residents' homes aren't on the line, and no bonds default, because none were ever sold. This is a long-term budget problem, not a collapse. But it would limit what the Town can do for years, and for a decade or more without deep cuts.

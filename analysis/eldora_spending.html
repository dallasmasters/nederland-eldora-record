As of the Nov. 9, 2026, warrant list, the Town had paid $1,254,931 in Eldora acquisition costs, and firms had confirmed another $1,623,777 owed, for about $2.9 million in all (who was paid and who is owed); the Town estimates about $2.76 million, not itemized. All of it is charged to the General Fund. No budget line was set for it, and until Nov. 17 no dollar limit was either, but the costs count against the General Fund’s 2026 appropriation, set for the fund as a whole, and staff forecast 2026 spending including them at $180,221 under it. The purchase agreement expired in April 2026, and the Town is pursuing a restructured deal and listing possible donations and partners. Staff’s Nov. 16 tables put the General Fund’s available balance at −$2,180,816 at the end of 2026 and −$2,528,102 at the end of 2027; the Nov. 9 forecast was −$1,948,512 and −$2,132,122, and with this page's itemized total its 2026 figure would be about −$2,068,634. To close the gap, staff now propose transfers from the Community Center, Water, and Sewer Funds, citing user fees and the Board's power to transfer money between funds; for Dec. 1 they no longer propose amending the 2026 budget, saying the General Fund is likely to stay within its 2026 appropriation. By this site's check, the plan's split asks more of the Sewer Fund than its stated limit and its cash, relies on Community Center money that voters approved for the Community Center, and closes only if nearly all of the money in all three funds can be used. Resolution 2026-31, adopted Nov. 17, limits further acquisition spending, requires monthly cost reports and a Dec. 15 accounting, and ends the Town’s pursuit if a milestone passes without an extension, the first a term sheet by Feb. 15, 2027; the Interim Town Manager told eight firms Nov. 20 to pause billable work, and staff report about $170,000 in potential fee reductions. Records released Nov. 26 show that Hogan Lovells, paid $148,795, is Alterra Mountain Company's counsel on the Ikon agreement, whose fees the Town shares with POWDR. The page also covers what would change if the purchase closes, including the bond payment schedules and property taxes for the fire and library districts; what the Town has told the public; what the records searched don't show; and every Board vote on the acquisition, each unanimous among the trustees present.

Summary of findings based on records searched

  1. The Town has paid $1,254,931, all charged to its General Fund, at first as money it expected to recover from revenue bonds that haven't been sold; the Town's Nov. 9, 2026, forecast now counts the costs as spending. The purchase agreement has expired. The Town says it is pursuing a restructured sale-leaseback, but as of Oct. 22, 2026, the lease “has not been drafted” (Town response).
  2. The costs come to about $2.9 million by this page's itemized total, and the Town now counts them as spending whether or not the sale closes ($4,202 per household): $1,254,931 paid and $1,623,777 that firms confirmed owed. The Town estimates about $2.76 million, not itemized (the bottom line). The General Fund has little or no cash of its own, so paying would use other funds' pooled cash, mostly the Water and Sewer Funds' (Could other funds cover it?). The Town's own Nov. 9 forecast already charges its estimate to the General Fund, ending 2026 at −$1,948,512; with this page's itemized total, the figure would be about −$2,068,634. Neither bankruptcy nor dissolving the Town would remove the costs.
  3. To cover the deficit, staff propose transfers from the Community Center, Water, and Sewer Funds; by this site's check, the plan doesn't close as presented. The Nov. 16 memo lists up to $800,000 from the Community Center and $2.3 million from the utilities, from “user fees” (the Nov. 16 plan). Voters approved the Community Center's sales tax for the Community Center, most recently in April 2022, and dedicated the utilities' sales tax to their debt; the Town's own Sept. 30 balance sheet and the 2025 audit report the whole Community Center Fund as restricted, and staff's own Oct. 30 spreadsheet assumed it was (what voters approved). The memo's 75% Sewer share exceeds Sewer's stated limit and its cash, and without the Community Center money the utilities' maximum falls short of the gap (this site's check).
  4. Some Eldora invoices have gone unpaid for more than a year. Of what is owed, $246,395 had been invoiced and was unpaid as of Oct. 16, 2026, and wasn't paid on the Nov. 9 warrant list, including Williams Weese Pepple & Ferguson invoices dating to Aug. 11, 2025, with $84,457 more than 90 days past due on its Sept. 9 statement (statement), and Dickinson Wright invoices dating to Oct. 29, 2025. Dickinson Wright's emails listing its unpaid invoices went to the Mayor from November 2025, and the Mayor replied to the January email, “Thanks Sarah”; from May 2026 they also went to the Town Manager and the Town's accounts payable (emails). None of these unpaid invoices is on a warrant list searched. A Hogan Lovells invoice of May 29, 2026, for $8,943, which POWDR says is “directly attributable to the Town,” is also unpaid; the Town Attorney sent it to the Town's accounts payable Oct. 29, and it isn't in the firms' confirmations (invoices and emails).
  5. No budget covers the acquisition costs, and every Board vote on the acquisition was unanimous among the trustees present. Neither the 2025 nor the 2026 budget includes them. The warrant lists the Board approved showed the payments and the account's running balance. The Board made 17 decisions and 32 votes to meet in closed session on the acquisition since Sept. 3, 2024.
  6. Lawyers and consultants received most of the money. The largest payees are Widner Juran LLP ($293,931), POWDR Corp ($197,079), Plante Moran ($161,656), Hogan Lovells ($148,795), and SE Group ($134,231). $646,194 went to firms whose role is stated in a Board document, $338,389 to regular Town vendors linked to the acquisition only by account coding, and $270,348 to firms that appear in no Board document searched except the warrant lists. The resolutions delegating authority to hire them set no dollar limits.
  7. The Town hasn't itemized its estimate, now about $2.76 million. Asked for the records behind its Sept. 30 estimate of about $2.5 million, the Town released on Oct. 29, 2026, the firms' confirmations of what they were owed as of Oct. 16, but no worksheet for the estimate (Town response). Most of what is owed, $1,174,083, not yet invoiced, is to real estate counsel Brownstein Hyatt Farber Schreck, whose agreement bills when the transaction is completed or terminated. The response includes no confirmation from Hogan Lovells, Plante Moran, or POWDR (who is owed).
  8. If the purchase closes, the fire and library districts would lose part of their tax base. By this page's estimate, if the sellers' property became tax-exempt Town property, the Nederland Fire Protection District would stop collecting $97,000 to $107,000 a year (about 6% of its property tax) and the Nederland Community Library District $29,000 to $32,000 (about 5%) (Property taxes on the resort).

Why I made this page

I built this page while researching the Town's public records on its website and CivicWeb portal. Like many residents, my views on the Eldora acquisition have changed over time, but from the start I worried about the cost of pursuing it and the risk to the Town if the deal fell through, since those costs would be repaid only if the sale closed. This page tracks those costs from the Town's own records and gathers information scattered across thousands of documents, to help us figure out the best path forward.

My opinions about what will happen and what the Town should do are on a separate page, so that they stay apart from the record. Because the sale is still more likely to fail than close, I recommend that the Board limit spending on the acquisition, set firm deadlines for ending the effort that it can extend only by a vote, and focus on the Town's finances and the 2027 budget. I've drafted a resolution that would do that; the Town Attorney revised it as Resolution 2026-31, and I recommend adopting it with the amendments on my opinion page. On my rough review of the numbers, any future attempt should start from a cash price of about $70 million or less.

— Dallas Masters, Nederland resident · Questions and corrections: corrections@eldorarecord.org

The acquisition in brief

POWDR put Eldora Mountain Resort, the ski area just west of Nederland, up for sale in 2024. The Town plans to buy it through a new Town-owned business, the Mountain Recreation Enterprise, and pay with revenue bonds repaid only from the resort's income, not taxes. In the May 2026 draft bond documents, part of the price would have been paid in bonds to POWDR, repaid only after the senior bonds. A January 2026 draft agreement would also have the Town pay POWDR at least $1 million a year for two years of transition services after closing. Its stated reasons are local control and long-term revenue (Ord. 873, FAQ 6.0), including "addressing recurring budget shortfalls through a more stable revenue base" (FAQ 7.0). The plan is being restructured (see below).

  1. November 2024: the Town said it was pursuing the purchase, and on Nov. 19 the Board first discussed it in open session, as a discussion item with no vote (memo, minutes). Closed sessions on purchasing “property for recreational activities” began Sept. 3, 2024 (Board votes).
  2. July 2025: non-binding term sheet signed with POWDR (FAQ 3.0).
  3. January 2026: Board approved a $120 million purchase and up to $225 million in bonds (Ord. 882, Ord. 885).
  4. April 30, 2026: the purchase agreement's closing deadline passed without a closing (APA §7.1(d), FAQ 7.0).
  5. July 19, 2026: the Board's authority to sell the bonds ran out with no bonds sold (Ord. 885 §5(c)).
  6. September 30, 2026: the Town said the agreement had expired but it is still pursuing the purchase with restructured financing (Town update).

Where things stand.

  • The agreements. No purchase agreement is in force; a few terms of the expired one continue, including that each side pays its own costs (APA §7.2, §8.2). The Town remains bound by its confidentiality agreement with POWDR, dated Jan. 10, 2025, and signed for the Town by the Town Administrator. It covers information POWDR provides and anything the Town prepares from it, and ends two years after signing or at closing, whichever comes first, except for material POWDR identifies in writing as a trade secret (§5(a)). Lenders, investors, and financial advisors may see the material only with POWDR's written consent (§1(a)). If Colorado law requires disclosure, the Town agreed, as far as the law allows, to notify POWDR first and to disclose only after POWDR's written consent (§1(d)). Amendments on July 8 and July 10, 2025, signed by the Mayor, let the Town disclose 12 listed items, such as that a term sheet exists and the financing structure, but not the term sheet's contents (agreement and amendments).
  • The new deal. The Town's Sept. 30 update describes a revised plan: a "sale-leaseback structure tied to the asset," less debt, and about $20 million in philanthropy for capital projects, with the State of Colorado the first potential contributor, "at the beginning stages" (Town update). Draft Resolution 2026-31 says the Town has been restructuring the deal “in response to feedback from prospective bondholders”: to “reduce the overall amount of debt, to model interest rates and poor snow years more conservatively using the most recent season’s actual results,” and to separate discretionary capital improvements from the order of bond payments. It says the new deal “requires additional work before a term sheet can be presented to the Board” (Res. 2026-31). Under an Oct. 16 overview, the Enterprise would transfer legal title to the land and improvements to the bond trustee, which would lease the resort back to the Enterprise; after a default, the trustee could act “without first completing a traditional foreclosure process,” and “those remedies could affect who operates or leases the resort.” Title would return once the bonds are paid (sale-leaseback overview). On Oct. 22 the interim Town Manager wrote that the lease “has not been drafted” and provided the newest draft indenture, dated as of May 1, 2026, before the sale-leaseback plan (Town response); no new bond amount or pledges are in the records searched. A new agreement, lease, or bond ordinance would each need Board approval at a public meeting. Stifel's advisory agreement, extended in July 2026, covers municipal leases as well as bonds (agreement).
  • Who is doing the work. An Oct. 20 staff memo says the Town is pursuing the purchase "at the previously negotiated purchase price of $120 million" and exploring "revenue bonds, governmental grants, foundation funding, and private contributions." It says this work "is primarily being performed by the Mayor" and that "the Town is not paying outside consultants to perform this work" (Oct. 20 memo).
  • Donations and partners. Two documents in the Nov. 17, 2026, packet, also posted on the Town website, list possible ways to raise money for the purchase and later improvements. One says that, “as part of the acquisition financing, the Town could recognize people who make substantial contributions,” with options such as naming rights, reserved parking, ski storage, and long-term or “lifetime” passes. It says long-term passes need advance approval by the Forest Service's Regional Forester, may number no more than 200, can't last beyond the term of the special use permit, and must use their proceeds “for qualifying capital improvements” (donor options). The other lists possible support from governments, residents, major donors, foundations, land trusts, and businesses, and says “the Town welcomes additional ideas” (partner support). Neither gives dollar amounts, a goal, a timeline, or named donors or partners (Nov. 17 Eldora memo).
  • Town staff. The Town Manager resigned in August 2026, and the Town's previous Town Manager returned as interim Town Manager on Sept. 25, 2026 (Aug. 18 minutes, p. 4); her contract runs through Feb. 28, 2027, and recruiting a permanent Town Administrator is expected to begin after the 2027 budget is developed (Oct. 20 report, reply to public comment). The Town Treasurer retired in 2026 (July 21, 2026, Town Manager report; Sept. 15, 2026, budget memo); since July 2026, the Town’s monthly treasurer reports have been issued by Plante Moran as Town Treasurer (July 21 report). The Board packets searched have a report for July (Sept. 1 packet) and then one for September (Nov. 17 packet); none has an August report, and the Sept. 15, Oct. 6, Oct. 20, and Nov. 9 packets have no monthly financial report (July report, September Treasurer report). The Town Manager's Nov. 17 report lists “filling critical Finance Department vacancies (Finance Support Specialist and Town Treasurer)” among her immediate priorities (Nov. 17 report).
Spent and owed

How the costs were recorded, and how that changed. When the Town pays an Eldora acquisition bill from the General Fund, the cash leaves either way, but the books can record the payment in one of two ways. The General Fund has already relied on other funds' cash: the 2025 audit reports its "negative cash balance of $757,482" as accounts payable, covered by the Town's pooled cash (2025 audit). Colorado law sets the rules a deficit would face. A local government's budget may not "provide for expenditures in excess of available revenues and beginning fund balances," and no one may spend or contract to spend "in excess of the amounts appropriated" (C.R.S. 29-1-103(2), 29-1-110). The 2026 General Fund appropriation is $5,674,176, set for the fund as a whole (Res. 2025-28), and the Eldora costs count against it as 2026 spending. Staff’s Nov. 16 forecast of 2026 spending, including them, is $5,493,955, which is $180,221 under it (budget tables). Part of the room is in the grant budget: the General Fund’s grant lines, budgeted at $2,250,000, had spent about $100,000 by the Nov. 9 warrant list, and the grant revenue that would have paid for them didn’t come (Nov. 9 warrants). A budget that starts from a deficit would have to close it with more revenue, less spending, or transfers. For scale, $1.6 million is about 50% of the General Fund's 2026 operating budget of $3,236,824, and $2.9 million is about 89% (budget summary). Under TABOR, the Town needs voter approval in advance for "any new tax, tax rate increase," or mill levy increase, and for any "multiple-fiscal year direct or indirect district debt or other financial obligation" not backed by cash reserves pledged for all future payments. The TABOR emergency reserve, $147,000 of the General Fund's balance, is "for declared emergencies only," and an emergency "excludes economic conditions, revenue shortfalls" (Colo. Const. art. X, §20(2)(c), (4), (5)). At the General Fund's 2017–2023 average gain of $126,000 to $199,000 a year, rebuilding the Nov. 16 forecast's available-balance deficit of about $2.2 million would take roughly 11 to 17 years (this page's calculation). That is a measure of scale, not a forecast: the General Fund's balance fell by $265,953 in 2024 and $192,241 in 2025, and the Town's Nov. 9 forecast shows the fund spending more than it takes in, before any Eldora costs, in 2026 (−$220,004) and 2027 (−$183,610) (year by year).

The State Auditor's fiscal-health benchmark for counties and municipalities is an unrestricted General Fund balance of at least two months of expenditures, a ratio of 0.167, and it says a negative unrestricted balance "is not sustainable, and a government should address the situation as soon as possible" (State Auditor, 2013, p. 13). Nederland's ratio was about 0.26 at the end of 2025; at the end of 2026 in the Town's forecast, it would be about −0.65 (this page's calculation). In the audits searched from 2008 through 2024, the General Fund appears as a lender to other funds, not a borrower; in 2008 it "temporarily covered the negative cash balances" of two other funds (2008 audit), and 2025 is the first year an audit reports it with negative cash. The Town's formal loans between funds have gone to the Downtown Development Authority, not the General Fund (below). The records searched don't show how the budget law's limit applies when a fund begins the year with a negative balance, which read literally could require closing the gap within one budget year; that is a question for the Town attorney or auditor.

Bankruptcy and dissolution. Neither would remove these costs. A local government can file for federal municipal bankruptcy (Chapter 9) only if its state specifically authorizes it (U.S. Courts). The Colorado statute that does so names only an “irrigation or drainage district” (C.R.S. 37-32-102), and no statute searched authorizes a town. A town can end its incorporation only if 25% of its registered voters petition and two-thirds vote to do so, and only after it “has made proper provisions for the payment of all of its indebtedness” and its contracts, levying taxes if needed (C.R.S. 31-3-101, 31-3-104). A town that goes five years without holding elections or maintaining a government can be declared abandoned, but its debts “shall not be abrogated,” and the county takes over to pay them (C.R.S. 31-3-201, 31-3-202). The Eldora bonds would not be a Town debt: the January 2026 draft indenture says they “shall not constitute or become an indebtedness, a debt, or a liability or a charge against the general credit or taxing power of the Town” (draft indenture §7.01); the sale-leaseback documents aren't public. By this page's reading, the acquisition costs are a General Fund budget problem the Board would address through its budgets, not a threat to the Town's existence.

What voters approved for these funds' sales tax

Part of each fund's income is a share of the Town's sales tax. Town Code Sec. 4-57, as rewritten by the Board in 2022 (Ord. 831), sends 52.95% of the sales tax to the General Fund “to be used for any municipal purpose,” 11.76% each to the Water and Sewer Funds, and 17.65% to the Community Center Fund, without stating a purpose for those shares (Town Code, Ord. 831). Earlier versions, and the ballot questions behind them, did:

  • The Community Center. Voters approved a 0.42% sales tax in 1997. In November 2002 they extended it “for the acquisition, renovation, repair, operation and maintenance” of the Community Center building; the ballot question refers to “the Town sales tax which are dedicated to the Community Center” (2002 ballot notice). In April 2022, voters approved continuing it without expiration for the 2002 debt and “funding the improvement, renovation, and repair to the community center and the operation and maintenance of the community center and other community center purposes as approved by the trustees” (Res. 2022-08; the 2023 budget book says voters “overwhelmingly approved” it, p. 30). Until 2022, the Town Code said the Community Center's share is “used exclusively” for the building (Ord. 703). The Town's own Sept. 30, 2026, balance sheet shows the fund's $890,426 as “Restricted for Community Center” and its unrestricted fund balance as $0 (September financials, p. 9), as the 2025 audit did for its $830,528 (2025 audit, p. 20). Sales tax was about $404,000 of the fund's $583,000 in yearly revenue, and fitness, rental, and other fees about $140,000 to $157,000 (staff spreadsheet; 2026 budget, p. 158). The 2025 audit also states that “when both restricted and unrestricted resources are available for use, it is the Town’s practice to use restricted resources first, then unrestricted resources as they are needed” (2025 audit, PDF p. 30). The 2013 ordinance that refinanced the Community Center’s bonds applied the sales tax to the bonds “and after such payment, to other purposes authorized by law from time to time” (Ord. 719, Sec. 12).
  • Water and Sewer. In November 1998, voters dedicated the utilities' share of the sales tax to water debt and then sewer debt; in April 2012 they split it “one half … to pay water debt … and one half … to pay sewer debt” (Ord. 703, Ord. 711). Ord. 711 says this sales tax was “pledged to the repayment” of the utilities' state loans. The 2026 budget says each fund's $275,000 of sales tax is “designated” for its debt (2026 budget, pp. 138–139). User fees, the source the Nov. 16 memo names, were not part of these votes.
  • What the Town Code says about changing these uses. Sec. 4-58 lets the Board amend the sales tax article without a vote, “except as to the sales tax rate,” the items taxed, “and the use of the sales tax revenues provided for in Sections 4-32 and 4-57.” Sec. 4-32 says amounts in the Sales Tax Capital Improvement Fund “shall be used solely to provide capital improvements or to pay debt services” on obligations issued for them (Town Code).
  • The 2018 sewer debt vote. In April 2018, voters approved up to $2 million of debt for wastewater biosolids improvements. The ballot issue let the Town pledge “the rates, fees, payments and charges of the wastewater system and any portion of the Town’s sales tax as hereafter determined by the Board of Trustees” (April 3, 2018, sample ballot, Ballot Issue 2B).

This page doesn't decide whether a transfer would be lawful. Whether Sec. 4-11 or the 2022 code rewrite can change a purpose voters approved, and whether the transferred money can be traced to user fees, are questions for the Town Attorney; the Nov. 17 executive session lists “applicable Colorado budget requirements” among its topics.

Could the utility funds lend or transfer money to the General Fund?

The Water and Sewer Funds hold most of the Town's pooled cash (the Town's funds), and they have lent money before. From 2012 through 2016 they lent the Downtown Development Authority $842,500 in four loans: $482,500 from the Water Fund in 2012, repaid by 2016; $7,000 from the Water Fund in 2014 and $13,000 from the Sewer Fund in 2015, each due within months and not outstanding in the year-end audits; and $340,000 from the Water Fund in 2016, repaid in 2017 (2016 audit, 2017 audit). The 2016 audit reported the $340,000 in the authority's fund as “Proceeds from Issuance of Debt,” a financing source that raised that fund's balance, and the authority's budget schedule listed it as “Proceeds from Loan.” The audit describes the authority as “a blended component unit of the Town.” Staff's 2026 analysis treats a loan to the General Fund differently, as leaving its balance unchanged (the Town's options); the records searched don't say whether the Town's auditor would report a loan to the General Fund as the 2016 audit did. Each of the 2014–2016 loans was approved by Board resolution with an interest rate, a deadline, and repayment from the authority's tax increment revenue, and each resolution found that the lending fund could provide the money “without hampering the operations or replacement schedules” of that fund. The resolutions count the loans against borrowing voters approved in 2012 (Res. 2014-33, 2015-06, 2016-20). In the audits searched from 2008 through 2025, the General Fund has never been the borrower on a loan from another fund.

Referendum
Registered voters can petition to send most ordinances to a public vote. The petition needs signatures from 5% of registered voters within 30 days after the ordinance is published, and the ordinance is on hold until the Board repeals it or voters decide (C.R.S. 31-11-105). Residents used this in 2024 on Ordinance 859, the TEENS child care approval (Town memo). This applies to ordinances approving a purchase or bonds, even though the bonds themselves need no election.
Initiative
Registered voters can propose an ordinance by a petition signed by at least 5% of registered voters. The Board must adopt it unchanged within 20 days or send it to the voters (C.R.S. 31-11-104).
Elections
The next regular municipal election is in April 2028 (Town memo). A vacant trustee seat was filled by appointment in September 2026 (Res. 2026-25). Colorado's constitution also allows recall of elected officials by petition (Colo. Const. art. XXI).

Nederland is a statutory town, and it applied these state petition rules in 2024. The Board has discussed converting to home rule, which would let a charter set different rules (Sept. 15 minutes). How this research was done

This page was researched and built by Claude, an AI model made by Anthropic, working in Claude Code at the direction of Dallas Masters. The research began in October 2026. Claude collected, read, and analyzed the documents. The researcher chose the questions and decided what went on the page.

Show how the records were collected, read, and checked
  • Collecting the records. All 16,511 documents listed in the Town's CivicWeb Document Center were cataloged. 6,754 of them were selected and downloaded:
    • Board of Trustees agendas, packets, agenda attachments, and minutes from January 2023 on, including executive-session agendas and the February 2026 town hall on the acquisition.
    • Agendas, packets, and minutes of the Town's other boards and commissions from January 2024 on: the Downtown Development Authority, Planning Commission, Sustainability Advisory Board, Parks, Recreation, and Open Space Advisory Board, and Board of Zoning Adjustment (1,422 documents).
    • All years of resolutions, ordinances, finance documents, and staff reports.
  • Reading the text. Text was taken directly from each PDF where possible. The 9,533 pages that were scanned images were read with optical character recognition (OCR, the Tesseract program). All 65,952 pages went into a searchable database, so every finding can be traced to a document and page.
  • Tracing the payments. Every warrant list (the bills the Board approves at each meeting) was parsed for payments to the General Fund account 10-00-1150, "Due From," and, from the Nov. 9, 2026, list, account 10-10-7000, "Eldora Expense." The "Due From" account's running balance printed on each list was reconciled against the listed invoices. That check found three amounts posted between lists. The Town's ledger for the account, from a public records response, identifies the entries that make up each one; it was checked entry by entry against the warrant lists, including the earlier lists where moved invoices were first paid.
  • Checking each payee. Every payee was searched for across the whole archive, including contracts, engagement letters, staff memos, and minutes. Their other Town billings were compared to see whether "Due From" charges were kept separate. Each payee was then rated by whether a Board document states its role in the Eldora acquisition.
  • Reading the contracts. The draft Asset Purchase Agreement, the Transition Services Agreement, the bond ordinance and the delegation resolutions were read for cost-sharing, refund, termination, and spending limits.
  • The Town's website. The Town's Eldora FAQs and announcements are posted on its website; only FAQ Round 2 also appears in CivicWeb, in the Jan. 7, 2025, Board packet. Current posts (FAQ 6.0, FAQ 7.0, the September 30, 2026, update, and the auditor request for proposals) came from nederlandco.gov. Earlier FAQs were on the Town's previous website, which no longer serves them, so they came from the Internet Archive. FAQ 5.0 could not be found. The 2022, 2023, and 2025 audits are also posted only on the Town website. They are scanned PDFs and were read with OCR. All were added to the same searchable database as the Board records.
  • Records from public records requests. Records the Town released in response to an open records (CORA) request are used like the Town's other records. The Town's ledger for account 10-00-1150, "Due From," came from its Sept. 25, 2026, response to a resident's request, which the requester shared with this project. The ledger matches every warrant-list payment on this page and the account's running balance to the cent. Records released to residents are posted with this page; requesters are named only with their consent, and their contact details are not posted. These records were added to the same searchable database as the Board records.
  • Meeting videos. The Town posts recordings of Board meetings on its YouTube channel. To link each decision to the moment it comes up in the recording, the recordings of the meetings with Eldora decisions were transcribed by machine (whisper.cpp), and YouTube's automatic captions were searched for other meetings. The transcripts were used only to find those times, and each link was checked by watching the recording; they are not a source for anything on this page, because they are unofficial and contain errors. The minutes are the official record. The one exception is the Town's Nov. 9, 2026, budget presentation, which wasn't in the meeting packet: screenshots of its slides from the recording are posted and cited as the Town's presentation.
  • Outside sources. State statutes and the Colorado Constitution are cited for CSAFE, for the budget and TABOR rules a General Fund deficit would face, and under Get involved; they come from the official 2024 Colorado Revised Statutes. The State Auditor's 2013 fiscal-health analysis for counties and municipalities is cited for its General Fund reserve benchmark. The section on property taxes uses Boulder County records: the Assessor's account data (Oct. 17, 2026, download), its 2025 mill levy file and certifications of value to each district, and the Treasurer's account pages for the three largest accounts, printed Oct. 27, 2026. Accounts were matched to the sellers by owner name and mailing address. Everything else on this page comes from the Town's own records, website, and Facebook page. FAQ 7.0 refers to a BusinessDen article; the article itself is not used as a source.
  • Reading the minutes. The signed "Minutes - Adopted" scans carry a built-in text layer that often garbles names and votes. All 123 adopted Board minutes since 2023 were therefore re-read with OCR at high resolution. Names were checked against the page images wherever they were unclear. Four sets of minutes were never posted as separate adopted minutes; for those (Oct. 21, 2025, regular and special, Oct. 30, 2025, and the Sept. 15, 2026, special meeting), the drafts in the next meeting's packet were used.
  • Finding the motions. 500 motions were extracted with mover, seconder, outcome, roll call, absences and late arrivals. Votes on the acquisition were chosen by reading each candidate item's staff memo and attachments, because a search for "Eldora" alone also returns items that mention it only in passing. Every executive session whose motion cites the purchase of property for recreational activities was included.
  • Checking for gaps. Agendas were checked for executive sessions on the purchase that have no motion in any minutes. Those are listed separately, along with one whose agenda is marked canceled. No minutes were found for four special meetings (Dec. 23, 2024, and May 30, July 21, and July 29, 2025).
  • Limits. When this page says something isn't in the records, it means the records searched; it may be documented elsewhere. OCR can misread scanned text, and an AI can misread or misjudge a document. Every figure and claim on this page links to its source document, so check those before relying on them. Executive sessions are confidential and the public record has gaps, so this page cannot show everything the Board knew or decided.
